Class 12 maths is the paper where a steady plan matters more than raw talent. Calculus alone carries 35 of the 80 theory marks, the question paper design has not changed for 2026-27, and the marking scheme rewards clean, complete working. Students who lose marks rarely lack understanding of the big ideas. They lose marks through algebra slips inside long integrals, missing justification in continuity questions, wrong limits in area problems, and too little practice under time pressure.

This guide is written for the 2026-27 batch, who sit the board exam in 2027. It covers the official syllabus and unit-wise weightage, the question paper design, what has been taken out of the syllabus in recent years (so you do not waste time on it), internal assessment, a chapter-by-chapter guide to all 13 NCERT chapters, a month-by-month plan, a last-30-days checklist, the mistakes that cost marks, and how to balance the board paper with JEE, CUET and other entrance exams. Everything about the syllabus and paper pattern below has been checked against CBSE's 2026-27 curriculum and sample question paper.

CBSE Class 12 Maths 2026-27 at a glance

Subject code – Mathematics (041). Applied Mathematics (241) is a separate subject with its own syllabus and is not covered here.

Marks – 80 for the theory paper and 20 for internal assessment, 100 in total.

Paper – 3 hours, 38 compulsory questions in five sections (A to E), with internal choice in some questions.

Syllabus – six units and 13 NCERT chapters across Part I and Part II of the Class 12 textbook.

No calculators – calculators are not allowed, and π is taken as 22/7 where required unless stated otherwise.

No change – CBSE's 2026-27 sample paper states that there is no change in the question paper design and assessment pattern for this session.

Class 12 has one main board exam. The two-exam system that CBSE introduced from 2026 applies to Class 10, not Class 12. In 2026, Class 12 theory exams began on 17 February. CBSE has not yet published the 2027 date sheet, so take the exact dates from cbse.gov.in when they are announced.

Three things follow from this table. First, calculus is 35 marks, close to 44% of the theory paper, so the five calculus chapters deserve the largest share of your practice time. Second, the "small" units are not small in practice: Relations and Functions, Algebra and Probability together give 26 marks, and much of that is dependable scoring if you practise the standard question types. Third, Vectors and 3D Geometry carry 14 marks for a fairly limited set of methods, which makes them some of the best-value marks in the paper.

The curriculum also says there is no chapter-wise weightage within a unit and that care should be taken to cover all chapters. Do not skip a chapter because it looks light. Questions in one chapter often lean on another; integration needs inverse trigonometry, area needs integration, and differential equations need both.

What older guides include that the current syllabus does not

Many guidebooks, coaching notes and older blog posts, including an earlier version of this one, still list topics that the current CBSE syllabus does not include. If you are using older material, skip these unless your entrance exam needs them:

  • Tangents and normals, and approximations using derivatives, are not in the current Application of Derivatives syllabus. The 2026-27 list is rate of change, increasing and decreasing functions, and maxima and minima.
  • Rolle's theorem and the Mean Value Theorem are not listed under Continuity and Differentiability.
  • Random variables, probability distributions, mean and variance of a random variable, and the binomial distribution are not in the Probability syllabus. The current list stops at Bayes' theorem.
  • Properties of determinants and elementary row operations for finding an inverse are not listed; inverses are found with the adjoint.
  • Formation of differential equations is not listed; the syllabus starts from order, degree, and general and particular solutions.
  • Planes (equations of a plane, angle between planes, distance of a point from a plane) are not in the Three Dimensional Geometry syllabus, which covers lines only.
  • The scalar triple product is not in Vector Algebra.
  • Composition of functions and invertible functions are not in the Class 12 Relations and Functions list.

The NCERT textbook currently in print matches this reduced syllabus. If a practice book is full of plane equations and binomial distribution questions, it was written for an older syllabus. Check the date on any material before spending hours on it.

Limits the syllabus sets

  • Non-commutativity of matrix multiplication and zero products of non-zero matrices are restricted to square matrices of order 2.
  • Determinants go up to 3 × 3 only, and systems of equations are solved in two or three variables where the solution is unique.
  • Area under curves covers lines, circles, parabolas and ellipses in standard form only.
  • Maxima and minima use the first derivative test (motivated geometrically) and the second derivative test "as a provable tool".
  • Linear differential equations are of the form dy/dx + Py = Q or dx/dy + Px = Q, where P and Q are functions of x (or y) or constants.
  • Linear programming uses the graphical method in two variables with up to three non-trivial constraints.

So 45% of the paper asks you to use a method in an unfamiliar setting or reason through something new. The sample paper shows what this looks like: a drone's circular field of view growing as it climbs (related rates), a water-quality index modelled by a quadratic in investment (maxima), laser beams in a mall as lines in space (shortest distance), fruit baskets priced through a 3 × 3 system (inverse of a matrix), and false alerts from four brands of smartwatch (Bayes' theorem). The mathematics is standard NCERT; the wrapping is new. Practise translating a story into a method.

How to handle each question type

MCQs – Do the working in the rough column. Options are often built on a common slip, such as a principal value outside its range or a sign error in a derivative. Substitute back when you can.

Assertion–reason – Judge each statement on its own, then ask whether the reason actually explains the assertion. The sample paper pairs a definite integral of |x − 2| with the even-function property, which is true but does not apply because |x − 2| is not even.

2-mark questions – Short but complete: formula, substitution, result. Typical items are a quick integral, a derivative to prove a relation, a vector magnitude, or writing constraints from a feasible region.

3-mark questions – Related rates, parametric or second derivatives, a particular solution of a differential equation, foot of the perpendicular on a line, or a graphical LPP.

5-mark questions – Equivalence relations or injective/surjective checks, a longer definite integral, area by integration, and Bayes' theorem problems.

Case studies – Read the passage once for the story and once for the numbers. The 1-mark parts are usually direct; the 2-mark part carries the internal choice, so read both options before you choose.

Chapter-by-chapter guide to all 13 chapters

For each chapter: what the board tests, the key results, the questions you should expect, and the errors that cost marks. The order follows the NCERT textbook, Part I then Part II.

1. Relations and Functions

What is tested: Types of relations (reflexive, symmetric, transitive, equivalence) and one-one (injective) and onto (surjective) functions.

Key results: A relation is an equivalence relation only if all three properties hold. To show a function is one-one, assume f(a) = f(b) and show a = b. To show it is onto, take any y in the codomain and find an x in the domain with f(x) = y.

Typical questions: Checking whether a relation on a set of numbers or ordered pairs is an equivalence relation (often 5 marks); deciding whether a function on real numbers is injective, surjective or bijective; MCQs on the number of relations with a given property.

Common errors: Proving reflexivity and symmetry with one example instead of in general. Forgetting that onto depends on the codomain: f(x) = x² + 5 from real numbers to [0, ∞) is not onto because values below 5 are never reached. Declaring a function one-one because "it looks increasing" without a proof.

2. Inverse Trigonometric Functions

What is tested: Definition, domain, range and principal value branch of the six inverse functions, and their graphs.

Key results: The principal value ranges, especially sin⁻¹ in [−π/2, π/2], cos⁻¹ in [0, π], tan⁻¹ in (−π/2, π/2), and sec⁻¹ in [0, π] − {π/2}. The domains of sec⁻¹ and cosec⁻¹ are (−∞, −1] ∪ [1, ∞).

Typical questions: 1-mark MCQs on principal values, domains of composite expressions such as sec⁻¹(2x), and identifying a graph. This chapter mostly appears in Section A, and its results feed into differentiation and integration later.

Common errors: Giving an answer outside the principal range, for example sin⁻¹(sin 2π/3) = 2π/3 instead of π/3. Solving domains with strict inequalities where the endpoints should be included. Memorising graphs without their endpoints and asymptotes.

3. Matrices

What is tested: Order, types, equality, transpose, symmetric and skew-symmetric matrices, addition and multiplication, and invertible matrices with the uniqueness of the inverse.

Key results: (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ. Every square matrix is the sum of a symmetric and a skew-symmetric matrix: A = ½(A + Aᵀ) + ½(A − Aᵀ). The diagonal entries of a skew-symmetric matrix are zero. AB is defined only when the columns of A equal the rows of B.

Typical questions: Finding unknowns in a symmetric matrix; checking which of AB and BA is defined; expressing a matrix as a sum of symmetric and skew-symmetric parts; order-of-product MCQs.

Common errors: Assuming AB = BA. Multiplying entry by entry. Writing (AB)ᵀ = AᵀBᵀ. Forgetting to check that the product is defined before starting.

4. Determinants

What is tested: Determinants up to 3 × 3, minors and cofactors, area of a triangle, adjoint and inverse, consistency of systems, and solving a system of two or three equations using the inverse of a matrix.

Key results: A⁻¹ = adj A / |A| when |A| ≠ 0. |kA| = kⁿ|A| for an n × n matrix. |adj A| = |A|ⁿ⁻¹. For AX = B: if |A| ≠ 0 there is a unique solution X = A⁻¹B; if |A| = 0, check (adj A)B to decide between no solution and infinitely many.

Typical questions: MCQs such as |adj(AB)| or |2A| given |A|; a 5-mark or case-study question setting up three equations from a word problem and solving them with A⁻¹, as in the sample paper's fruit-basket case study.

Common errors: Sign errors in cofactors (use the chequerboard of + and −). Forgetting to transpose the cofactor matrix to get the adjoint. Writing X = BA⁻¹ instead of A⁻¹B. Arithmetic slips in the final multiplication; always substitute the answer back into one equation.

5. Continuity and Differentiability

What is tested: Continuity and differentiability at a point, the chain rule, derivatives of inverse trigonometric functions, implicit differentiation, exponential and logarithmic functions, logarithmic differentiation, parametric forms and second-order derivatives.

Key results: f is continuous at a if the left-hand limit, right-hand limit and f(a) are all equal. Differentiability implies continuity, not the reverse. For y = f(x)^g(x), take logs first. For parametric forms, dy/dx = (dy/dt)/(dx/dt), and the second derivative is d/dt(dy/dx) divided by dx/dt.

Typical questions: Finding constants that make a piecewise function continuous or differentiable; derivatives using substitutions such as x = cos θ to simplify inverse trigonometric expressions; proving a relation involving second derivatives; logarithmic differentiation of xˢⁱⁿˣ-type functions.

Common errors: Checking only one side of the point. Differentiating the second derivative of a parametric function with respect to t and stopping there. Treating y as a constant in implicit differentiation. Using an inverse trigonometric substitution without stating the interval for which it is valid.

6. Application of Derivatives

What is tested: Rate of change of quantities, increasing and decreasing functions, and maxima and minima using the first and second derivative tests, including simple real-life problems.

Key results: f is increasing where f′(x) > 0 and decreasing where f′(x) < 0. For extrema, solve f′(x) = 0, then check the sign change of f′ or the sign of f″. For absolute extrema on a closed interval, compare values at critical points and endpoints.

Typical questions: Related rates (area of a circle, volume of a cone, a ladder sliding down a wall, the sample paper's drone camera); finding intervals of increase or decrease; optimisation problems such as maximum volume of a box or minimum surface area of a tin; case studies built on a profit or quality function.

Common errors: Differentiating with respect to the wrong variable in related rates. Forgetting units such as cm²/s. Not justifying whether a critical point is a maximum or minimum. In optimisation, not expressing the quantity in one variable before differentiating.

7. Integrals

What is tested: Integration as the inverse of differentiation, integration by substitution, partial fractions and by parts, the standard special integrals, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (without proof), and properties and evaluation of definite integrals.

Key results: The syllabus lists the standard forms: dx/(x² ± a²), dx/√(x² ± a²), dx/√(a² − x²), dx/(ax² + bx + c), dx/√(ax² + bx + c), (px + q)/(ax² + bx + c), (px + q)/√(ax² + bx + c), √(a² ± x²), √(x² − a²) and √(ax² + bx + c). Learn the definite-integral properties, especially ∫₀ᵃ f(x) dx = ∫₀ᵃ f(a − x) dx and the even/odd property over [−a, a].

Typical questions: 2-mark and 3-mark indefinite integrals; a 5-mark definite integral using the f(a − x) property; integrals of modulus functions split at the point where the expression changes sign.

Common errors: Forgetting + C in indefinite integrals. Not changing the limits after substitution. Choosing the wrong first function in integration by parts (use ILATE as a guide, not a law). Splitting partial fractions incorrectly for repeated factors. Using the even-function property on a function that is not even.

8. Application of Integrals

What is tested: Area under simple curves: lines, circles, parabolas and ellipses in standard form.

Key results: Area = ∫ |y| dx between the correct limits. The area of an ellipse x²/a² + y²/b² = 1 is πab, which is a useful check on your working. Where a region is bounded by a curve and a line, find the intersection points first.

Typical questions: A 5-mark area question, such as the region bounded by a parabola and a line, or the area of an ellipse or circle in one quadrant multiplied by symmetry.

Common errors: No sketch. Wrong limits because the intersection points were not solved. Negative area left unexplained. Integrating with respect to x when the region is far easier in y.

9. Differential Equations

What is tested: Order and degree, general and particular solutions, and solution by separation of variables, homogeneous equations of first order and first degree, and linear equations.

Key results: Degree is defined only when the equation is a polynomial in its derivatives. For homogeneous equations, substitute y = vx. For linear equations, the integrating factor is e^∫P dx, and the solution is y × IF = ∫(Q × IF) dx + C.

Typical questions: Order-and-degree MCQs; 3-mark particular solutions with a given initial condition; identifying the type of equation before solving.

Common errors: Stating a degree for an equation that is not polynomial in its derivatives. Losing the constant of integration or adding it twice. Not using the initial condition to find C. Writing the integrating factor as e^P instead of e^∫P dx.

10. Vector Algebra

What is tested: Magnitude and direction, direction cosines and ratios, types of vectors, position vectors, section formula, and the scalar (dot) and vector (cross) products with their geometric meaning.

Key results: a · b = |a||b| cos θ; a · b = 0 for perpendicular vectors. |a × b| = |a||b| sin θ gives the area of a parallelogram; half of it gives a triangle. a × b is perpendicular to both a and b. |a × b|² + (a · b)² = |a|²|b|².

Typical questions: Finding a unit vector perpendicular to two vectors; angle between vectors; projection of one vector on another; a missing magnitude given the dot and cross products, as in the sample paper.

Common errors: Swapping the order in the cross product and losing the sign. Forgetting to divide by the magnitude for a unit vector. Mixing up direction ratios and direction cosines.

11. Three Dimensional Geometry

What is tested: Direction cosines and ratios of a line, vector and Cartesian equations of a line, angle between two lines, skew lines, and the shortest distance between two lines.

Key results: Shortest distance between skew lines r = a₁ + λb₁ and r = a₂ + μb₂ is |(a₂ − a₁) · (b₁ × b₂)| / |b₁ × b₂|. For parallel lines, use |b × (a₂ − a₁)| / |b|. Lines are parallel when their direction ratios are proportional.

Typical questions: Foot of the perpendicular from a point to a line; angle between two lines; shortest distance, often as a case study; converting Cartesian form to vector form.

Common errors: Reading direction ratios from an equation that is not in standard form (for example, "2 − z" in the denominator form means the direction ratio has a negative sign). Using the skew-lines formula for parallel lines, where b₁ × b₂ = 0. Forgetting the modulus.

12. Linear Programming

What is tested: Terminology, graphical solution of problems in two variables, feasible and infeasible regions (bounded or unbounded), and optimal feasible solutions, with up to three non-trivial constraints.

Key results: The optimum of a linear objective over a bounded feasible region occurs at a corner point. For an unbounded region, check the half-plane given by the objective function to confirm that a maximum or minimum exists.

Typical questions: Writing constraints from a shaded region (2 marks); a full graphical solution (3 marks); relations between coefficients when the optimum occurs at two points.

Common errors: Shading the wrong side of a constraint. Missing a corner point. Declaring a maximum on an unbounded region without the extra check. Graphs drawn freehand without scale.

13. Probability

What is tested: Conditional probability, the multiplication theorem, independent events, total probability and Bayes' theorem.

Key results: P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B). A and B are independent if P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B). Total probability: P(E) = Σ P(Eᵢ)P(E | Eᵢ). Bayes: P(Eᵢ | E) = P(Eᵢ)P(E | Eᵢ) / Σ P(Eⱼ)P(E | Eⱼ).

Typical questions: Dice and card conditional-probability MCQs; "exactly one of two independent events" questions; a 5-mark Bayes' theorem problem with three or four sources, such as machines, bags or brands of device.

Common errors: Confusing independent events with mutually exclusive events. Reversing the condition in P(A | B). Not defining the events E₁, E₂, E₃ and A in words before using Bayes. Probabilities of the partition that do not add up to 1.

How to use NCERT, exemplar, sample papers and previous papers

The curriculum names the NCERT Class 12 textbooks (Part I and Part II), the NCERT Exemplar Problems and the NCERT Laboratory Manual as prescribed books. Use them in that order. Finish every NCERT exercise and miscellaneous exercise first, because board questions are built on them. Then use exemplar problems for the harder application questions. From December, move to the 2026-27 sample paper and its marking scheme, then recent previous board papers.

When you use a previous paper, check each question against the current syllabus. Papers from before 2023 include questions on planes, binomial distribution and other topics now outside the syllabus. Skip those, or replace them with a question from the same chapter that is still in the syllabus.

The last 30 days: a checklist

Solve one full paper every alternate day in exactly 3 hours.

Mark each paper yourself against the CBSE marking scheme.

Update the error log after every paper, then fix the top three errors.

Revise the formula sheet daily: derivatives, integrals, vectors, 3D.

Re-do every NCERT example in Integrals and Differential Equations.

Practise five area questions with sketches and limits written out.

Practise three Bayes' theorem problems with events defined in words.

Re-check principal value ranges and domain questions.

Practise one LPP graph a day with a ruler and scale.

Solve two case studies a day from the sample paper and exemplar.

Keep the final week for revision and rest, not new material.

Sleep properly the week before the paper; tired students make sign errors.

Step marking and answer presentation

CBSE marks Class 12 maths step by step. A correct method with an arithmetic slip near the end still earns most of the marks; a correct final answer with no working may earn very little. Here is a 3-mark differential equation answer written the way examiners like to see it:

Solve dy/dx + y/x = x², given y(1) = 1.

This is linear with P = 1/x and Q = x².

IF = e^∫(1/x)dx = e^(log x) = x (x > 0).

Solution: y · x = ∫x² · x dx = x⁴/4 + C.

Using y(1) = 1: 1 = 1/4 + C, so C = 3/4.

Hence xy = x⁴/4 + 3/4, or y = (x⁴ + 3)/(4x).

Each line is something an examiner can give marks for: identifying the type, the integrating factor, the general solution, and the particular solution.

Presentation rules that protect your marks

Write the formula or standard integral before substituting into it.

Draw every area and LPP figure with a ruler and label intersection points.

Define events in words before starting any probability question.

Box or underline the final answer, with units in rate questions.

Keep rough work in a margin column, not mixed into the answer.

Number every answer exactly as in the paper, including sub-parts.

If you attempt both internal choices, cross out the one you do not want marked.

Common mistakes that cost marks (and the fix for each)

These errors come up again and again in Class 12 answers and mock tests. Almost all of them are fixed by a habit, not by extra hours.

Principal value outside the range – Check every inverse trigonometric answer against its principal branch.

Assuming AB = BA – Keep the order of matrix products exactly as given.

Cofactor signs – Write the + − + chequerboard above the matrix before computing cofactors.

Adjoint not transposed – The adjoint is the transpose of the cofactor matrix; write that step.

X = BA⁻¹ – For AX = B, the solution is X = A⁻¹B.

One-sided continuity check – Always find the left-hand limit, right-hand limit and f(a).

Stopping early on parametric second derivatives – Divide by dx/dt after differentiating dy/dx with respect to t.

Missing units in related rates – Write cm/s, cm²/s or m³/min in the final line.

No justification for maxima or minima – State the sign change of f′ or the sign of f″.

Forgetting + C – Every indefinite integral needs it.

Limits not changed after substitution – Change them, or return to x before substituting.

Wrong partial fractions – Repeated linear factors need a term for each power.

Even-function property misused – Check f(−x) = f(x) before halving the interval.

Modulus not split – Find where the expression inside |…| changes sign and split the integral there.

Area without a sketch – Sketch first; it shows the limits and which curve is on top.

Degree stated for a non-polynomial equation – Say "degree not defined" when derivatives appear inside sin, log or similar.

Integrating factor written as e^P – It is e^∫P dx.

Cross product order swapped – a × b = −(b × a); keep the order consistent.

Direction ratios misread – Rewrite each line in standard form before reading direction ratios.

Skew-line formula for parallel lines – If b₁ × b₂ = 0, use the parallel-lines formula.

LPP region shaded wrongly – Test the origin (or another point) in each inequality.

Independent confused with mutually exclusive – Independent: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B). Mutually exclusive: P(A ∩ B) = 0.

Bayes without defined events – Write E₁, E₂, E₃ and A in words first; check the priors add to 1.

Too long on one integral – If it stalls for more than a few minutes, move on and return later.

No final check – Keep the last 10–15 minutes to find unattempted parts and obvious slips.

Balancing boards with JEE, CUET and other entrance exams

For most Class 12 maths students, the board exam is not the only paper that matters. The good news is that the overlap is large. Calculus, vectors, 3D geometry, matrices and probability are central to both the board paper and engineering entrance exams, and the CUET mathematics paper is built on the Class 11 and 12 NCERT syllabus. The differences are in depth, speed and scope.

Depth – Entrance papers use the same ideas at a higher difficulty and with less working shown. Board papers reward complete written steps. You need both skills, and they are practised differently.

Scope – Entrance syllabi are set by the conducting body, not CBSE, and can include topics no longer in the CBSE Class 12 syllabus, such as parts of 3D geometry or probability distributions. Check the current official syllabus for your exam before you drop anything.

Timing – JEE Main usually has sessions in January and April, close to the board exam. Plan so that board revision and entrance practice support each other rather than compete.

Class 11 topics – Entrance exams test Class 11 heavily (conic sections, sequences, binomial theorem, complex numbers). The board paper does not, except as tools inside Class 12 questions.

A workable pattern for JEE aspirants is to learn each chapter to board level first, then add entrance-level problems in the same week. From December, give the board paper priority on alternate days, because written presentation is a separate skill that entrance practice does not build. For CUET, board preparation already covers most of the syllabus; add timed MCQ practice from January.

If you also take Physics and Chemistry, the same balance applies. See our CBSE Class 12 Physics guide and CBSE Class 12 Chemistry guide for chapter-level notes and study plans in the same format.

When does a home tutor help?

Many students manage Class 12 maths with school teaching, NCERT and steady practice. A tutor is worth considering when something specific is getting in the way:

Weak Class 11 foundations – Gaps in trigonometry, limits or algebra keep appearing as errors in calculus.

Integration stalls – The student understands the methods but cannot decide which one to use on an unfamiliar integral.

Marks stuck at one level – Tests keep coming back at the same score and nobody has diagnosed why.

No feedback on written work – Mock papers are done but not marked against a marking scheme.

Board and entrance together – The student needs someone to plan both without one crowding out the other.

Late start – The student is behind the school schedule and needs a structured catch-up plan.

What a good Class 12 maths session looks like

A useful session is not a second school lecture. A good tutor usually checks homework and the error log first, spends most of the hour on problems the student solves while the tutor watches, and ends by setting targeted practice. They explain why a method works, insist on complete written steps, and every few weeks set a timed paper and mark it as a CBSE examiner would.

Frequently asked questions

Which chapters carry the most marks?

Calculus, at 35 of 80 marks, spread across five chapters. Within calculus, CBSE gives no chapter-wise split, so prepare all five. Integrals and their applications usually take the longest to master, so start them early.

Are tangents and normals still in the syllabus?

No. The 2026-27 Application of Derivatives syllabus covers rate of change, increasing and decreasing functions, and maxima and minima. Tangents, normals and approximations are not listed.

Is the binomial distribution in the Class 12 syllabus?

No. The Probability chapter covers conditional probability, the multiplication theorem, independent events, total probability and Bayes' theorem. Random variables and the binomial distribution are not in the current CBSE syllabus, though some entrance exams may still include them.
