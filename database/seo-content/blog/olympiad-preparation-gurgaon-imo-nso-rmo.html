Maths and science olympiads in India come in two very different kinds. School-level olympiads run by private organisers, such as the Science Olympiad Foundation's IMO and science olympiad, are multiple-choice tests taken in school and open from Class 1. The official national pathway, run by the Homi Bhabha Centre for Science Education (HBCSE) with teacher associations, starts with IOQM in maths and the National Standard Examinations in science, and leads to the international olympiads. Parents in Gurgaon (Gurugram) often mix the two up, and that changes how a child should prepare.

This guide explains what each kind of olympiad is, who can take it, what it actually tests, how to prepare class by class, how olympiad work connects to board exams and JEE, and where a tutor helps. I teach maths, so the maths pathway gets the most detail, but the same logic applies to science.

One naming point causes a lot of confusion. The Science Olympiad Foundation calls its maths test the "International Mathematics Olympiad (SOF IMO)". That is a different thing from the International Mathematical Olympiad, the global competition where India's team of six is chosen through the HBCSE pathway. Both are worth doing, but they are not the same ladder.

What are SOF's IMO and science olympiad?

The Science Olympiad Foundation (SOF) runs several olympiads through schools. Its maths olympiad (SOF IMO) is open to Classes 1–12 and its science olympiad, long known to parents as the NSO and now listed on SOF's site as the International Science Olympiad (ISO), is also open to Classes 1–12. Students cannot register individually: the school registers and the exam is held at the school.

For the SOF IMO, SOF describes a one-hour, multiple-choice Level 1 paper for all classes, with a Level 2 round for Classes 3–12 open to roughly the top 5% of Level 1 participants. SOF has published its 2026-27 Level 1 dates, and schools pick from these, so ask your child's school which date it has chosen.

What these tests reward: careful reading, logical reasoning and a solid grip of the school syllabus, slightly stretched. A student who is sound in class-level maths and practises the question style usually does well. They are a good first taste of competition for primary and middle-school children.

What is the official maths olympiad pathway?

According to HBCSE, the national mathematical olympiad runs in stages:

  1. IOQM (Indian Olympiad Qualifier in Mathematics). Conducted by the Mathematics Teachers' Association of India (MTAI). A three-hour paper with 30 questions, where each answer is a single-digit or two-digit number. IOQM 2026 was held on 6 September 2026 and was open to students in Classes 8–12 born between 1 August 2007 and 31 July 2014.
  2. RMO (Regional Mathematical Olympiad). A three-hour paper with six problems that need full written proofs. For this cycle, HBCSE lists RMO 2026 on 15 November 2026. A minimum IOQM score is one of the qualifying conditions, but not the only one.
  3. INMO (Indian National Mathematical Olympiad). The best RMO performers qualify. A 4.5-hour paper with six proof problems, listed for 17 January 2027.
  4. IMOTC (training camp). About 65 top INMO students are invited to a camp at HBCSE, where selection tests choose six students.
  5. IMO. Those six students represent India at the International Mathematical Olympiad.

Eligibility rules and qualification criteria are published each year on the HBCSE olympiad site and the IOQM site, so check them before your child registers for the next cycle. Our earlier guide to RMO and INMO preparation, syllabus and strategy goes deeper into the topics.

What about the science olympiads?

HBCSE describes a five-stage programme for physics, chemistry, biology, astronomy and junior science:

  • National Standard Examinations (NSE). The first stage, organised by the Indian Association of Physics Teachers (IAPT) with the chemistry and biology teachers' associations. Objective questions pitched at Class 12 level, or Class 10 level for junior science.
  • Indian National Olympiads (INO). Roughly the top 400 students in each subject from the NSEs, with objective and long-answer questions, run by HBCSE.
  • Orientation-cum-Selection Camps (OCSC) for about 40 students per subject, then pre-departure training for the selected team, and finally the international olympiads.

Check IAPT's announcements for the current year's NSE registration and dates, and HBCSE's site for the later stages.

What does each kind of olympiad actually test?

School-level olympiads test whether a child can apply the syllabus quickly and accurately, plus general reasoning. Speed and accuracy on multiple-choice questions matter.

IOQM tests problem-solving in number theory, algebra, geometry and combinatorics at pre-college level; HBCSE's syllabus expects students to know the NCERT maths of Classes 8 to 10 first, and it does not include calculus. The answers are numbers, but getting there usually takes clever reasoning rather than long calculation. Many strong board students find it hard at first because the questions do not look like textbook exercises.

RMO and INMO test proof-writing. A correct answer without a clear, complete argument earns little. This is the biggest jump in the whole pathway, and the skill that takes longest to build.

NSEs test deep understanding of concepts at the upper end of the school syllabus, often with twists that punish rote learning.

How should your child prepare, class by class?

ClassSensible focus
Classes 1–5School-level olympiads for fun and confidence. Puzzles, mental maths and reading questions carefully. No pressure, no heavy coaching.
Classes 6–7Continue school-level olympiads. Start non-routine problems: patterns, number puzzles, simple geometry proofs. Build the habit of explaining answers in writing.
Classes 8–9For keen students, begin IOQM-style problems in number theory, algebra, geometry and counting. Work through past papers slowly, then discuss solutions.
Classes 10–12Timed IOQM practice, then proof-writing for RMO. Science students can look at NSE past papers. Balance carefully with board exams and, where relevant, JEE or NEET.

A few habits help at every stage:

  • Fewer problems, more thinking. Spending 30 minutes on one hard problem teaches more than rushing through ten easy ones.
  • Write full solutions. Even for multiple-choice or numerical answers, writing the reasoning builds the proof skill needed later.
  • Use official past papers. HBCSE publishes past papers and syllabus notes on its olympiad site. Start there before buying extra books.
  • Review mistakes. Keep a notebook of problems your child could not solve and come back to them a week later.

Does olympiad preparation help with boards and JEE?

Yes, with one caution. Olympiad problem-solving builds exactly the habits JEE Advanced rewards: reading an unfamiliar problem, trying several approaches and not giving up. Algebra, inequalities, combinatorics and geometry from olympiad work feed directly into JEE maths, as our topic-wise JEE maths preparation guide shows. For board exams, the benefit is confidence and accuracy, and the ability to handle the application and case-based questions boards now include; our CBSE Class 10 maths preparation guide shows how those questions look.

The caution: olympiads and boards reward different things. Board exams need neat, complete, step-marked answers across the whole syllabus, and JEE covers calculus and topics that the maths olympiads do not. A student who spends every evening on RMO problems in Class 11 can fall behind in school physics and chemistry. Decide early what the main goal is, and fit olympiad work around it. How can a tutor help with olympiad preparation?

For school-level olympiads, most children need little more than a strong grasp of the school syllabus and some practice with the question style. A regular school tutor can cover that.

For IOQM, RMO and beyond, a tutor who enjoys problem-solving helps in ways that books cannot:

  • choosing problems at the right level, so your child is stretched but not stuck for weeks;
  • reading your child's written proofs line by line and showing where an argument has a gap;
  • teaching topics schools rarely cover in depth, such as modular arithmetic, inequalities and counting techniques;
  • keeping olympiad work in proportion with school and board preparation.

Proof-writing in particular is a skill best learned with feedback. Your child writes a solution, someone who knows what a complete argument looks like reads it, and the gaps get fixed. That is hard to do alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is SOF's IMO the same as the International Mathematical Olympiad?

No. SOF's IMO is a school-based multiple-choice olympiad run by the Science Olympiad Foundation. India's team for the International Mathematical Olympiad is chosen through the official HBCSE pathway: IOQM, then RMO, then INMO, then a training camp where six students are selected.

From which class can my child take IOQM?

IOQM 2026 was open to students in Classes 8 to 12 born between 1 August 2007 and 31 July 2014. Eligibility is published afresh each year on the IOQM and HBCSE olympiad websites, so check the current rules before registering for the next cycle.

When should serious olympiad preparation start?

For the official maths pathway, Class 7 or 8 is a sensible time for a keen student to start non-routine problem-solving, since IOQM opens from Class 8. Younger children benefit most from school-level olympiads, puzzles and a strong grip of basics, without pressure or heavy coaching.

Will olympiad preparation hurt my child's board exam marks?

It should not, if it is kept in proportion. Olympiad work builds reasoning that helps in boards and JEE, but boards need complete, well-presented answers across the full syllabus. Plan olympiad practice around school work, and ease off in the months before board exams.

Can my child prepare for olympiads online?

Yes. Olympiad preparation is mostly problem-solving and discussion of written solutions, which works well online with a shared whiteboard or photos of the notebook.
