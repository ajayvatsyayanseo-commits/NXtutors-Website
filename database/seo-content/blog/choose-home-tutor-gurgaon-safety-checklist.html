Letting a new adult into your home to teach your child is a big step, and most parents feel a small knot of worry about it, even when the tutor comes recommended. At the same time, you want someone who can actually teach, not just someone who seems pleasant at the door.

This checklist covers both safety and quality when hiring a home tutor in Gurgaon (Gurugram): what to check before the first visit, the practicalities of society gates and visitor entry, how to set up the first few sessions, safety for online classes, payments and receipts, what to agree in writing, the warning signs, and when to switch. There is a printable checklist near the end.

Before the first visit

Confirm identity

Ask for the tutor's full name, a phone number you have spoken on, and a government photo ID that you can see before or at the first visit. Check that the name matches the one you were given. A tutor who is reluctant to share basic identity details is not the right tutor, however good the profile looks.

Ask for references

Ask for one or two families the tutor has taught recently, ideally in the same class band. A short phone call is enough: How long did the tutor teach your child? Were they punctual? Did they keep you informed? Would you hire them again?

Check subject knowledge

You do not need to be a subject expert to do this. Ask the tutor how they would teach a topic your child finds hard, which chapters usually cause trouble in that class, and how they use past papers. Good tutors answer specifically and without jargon. For board-specific work, ask directly: "How many Class 10 ICSE students have you taught recently?" or "Have you taught the IB internal assessment?"

Use the demo properly

The demo is your clearest chance to judge teaching. Sit in for at least part of it, and watch how the tutor explains, checks understanding and handles mistakes. Our demo class checklist for parents lists exactly what to look for.

Society gates and visitor entry

Most families in Gurgaon live in gated societies or in independent floors with some form of guard or visitor log. Sort out entry before the first class, so the tutor does not spend twenty minutes at the gate and your child does not lose half the session.

  • Pre-approve the visit through your society's visitor app or by informing the guard, with the tutor's name and expected time.
  • For regular classes, ask the society office how frequent visitors are registered. Many societies have a process for regular staff and visitors; use it so entry is logged each time.
  • Share your tower and flat number, the correct gate to use, and where the tutor can park a two-wheeler or car.
  • Allow for traffic. School-run and office-hour traffic on the main roads can delay anyone. Agree what counts as late and how the tutor will let you know.

The visitor log is also useful to you: it gives you a record of each class, which helps if there is ever a question about attendance or fees.

An adult at home for the first sessions

For the demo and at least the first few regular classes, make sure a parent or another trusted adult is at home. This is sensible for every age, and important for younger children.

You do not need to sit in the room for the whole hour. Being within earshot, walking past occasionally and chatting with the tutor for five minutes at the end is usually enough. It helps you judge the teaching, and it helps your child settle with someone new.

Class in a common room

Hold tuition in a common area such as the dining table or a study corner in the living room, not in a child's bedroom with the door closed. A common room keeps things visible, is a better working space than a bed or a sofa, and sets a clear tone for everyone. Keep the door open if the only suitable desk is in a separate room.

Online safety for online classes

If some or all classes are online, the safety questions change but do not disappear:

  • Use a shared family laptop or tablet in a common room, not a phone behind a closed door.
  • Keep class links, messages and shared files on a channel a parent can see, such as a family number or a parent-added group.
  • Keep the camera on for both tutor and student, and agree that the tutor will not ask to move to private chats or new apps.
  • Sit beside younger children for the first ten minutes of each class until the routine settles.

Payments and receipts

  • Know the fee before the demo, and agree whether it is per hour or per class.
  • Prefer a traceable payment method, such as a bank transfer or UPI, over cash.
  • Ask for a receipt, or at least a written acknowledgement, for every payment.
  • Be cautious about large advance payments before a trial and a plan.
  • Keep a simple record of classes held each month so you can match them to what you paid.

What to agree in writing

A short message that both of you acknowledge is enough. It should cover:

ItemWhat to settle
TimingsDays, start time and class length
FeeRate per hour or class, and when it is paid
CancellationHow much notice either side gives, and whether a missed class is rescheduled
Holidays and examsWhat happens during school holidays, festivals and exam weeks
Progress updatesHow often you will hear about progress and in what form
Notice to stopHow much notice either side gives before ending classes
Review pointA date, usually after the first month, to review how it is going

Warning signs

Take these seriously. One is worth a conversation; two or more usually mean it is time to change.

  • The tutor asks for the class to be held behind a closed door, or suggests you do not need to be home.
  • They contact your child directly on personal apps or social media without your knowledge.
  • They ask your child to keep anything secret from you.
  • They are often late, on their phone during class, or end early.
  • They avoid giving ID, references or a receipt.
  • They push for a large advance payment or new charges not agreed earlier.
  • They promise specific marks or ranks.
  • Your child seems anxious before class or reluctant to talk about it.

If anything makes you feel your child is unsafe, stop the classes immediately. You do not need proof to protect your child.

Quality: when to switch

Safety comes first, but a tutor can be perfectly safe and still not be the right teacher. After about a month, consider switching if:

  • there is no plan, or the plan is not being followed;
  • homework is rarely set or checked;
  • you hear nothing about progress unless you ask;
  • your child still cannot explain what they learnt in recent classes;
  • confidence and test marks show no movement at all.

Switching is a normal part of finding the right fit, not a failure. Tell the next tutor what did not work so they can start in the right place.

Frequently asked questions

Should a parent always be at home during tuition?

For the demo and the first few classes, yes. After that, many families keep an adult at home as a routine, especially for younger children. It is a reasonable expectation, and a good tutor will not object.

What ID should I ask a home tutor for?

A government photo ID with a name that matches the one you were given, plus a phone number you have actually spoken on. Ask for references from recent families too.

Is it rude to ask a tutor for references?

No. Experienced tutors expect it. Keep the call with the reference short and specific: punctuality, communication and whether they would hire the tutor again.

How do I make online classes safe for my child?

Use a shared device in a common room, keep all class communication on a channel you can see, keep cameras on, and sit nearby for younger children, at least for the first part of each class.

What should I do if I feel uneasy about a tutor?

Stop the classes and trust your judgement. You do not need to explain in detail. If you found the tutor through NXTutors, tell the team and ask for another tutor from the shortlist; switching is free.
