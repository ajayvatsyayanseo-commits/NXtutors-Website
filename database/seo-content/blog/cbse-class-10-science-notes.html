Class 10 Science is three subjects sharing one paper. Chemistry wants balanced equations, Biology wants labelled diagrams and exact terms, and Physics wants clean numericals with units. Most students lose marks by treating all three the same way: reading the chapter, highlighting half of it and hoping the paper asks what they remember.

This guide is written the way I teach the subject. It covers what the official 2026-27 curriculum actually examines (several chapters from older guides are no longer on the board paper), how the paper is built, chapter-by-chapter notes with the reactions, diagrams and formulae that keep coming back, and a plan from April to the board exam. Use it alongside the NCERT textbook, not instead of it. Every board question can be answered from NCERT, and that is where your revision should start and end.

What is examined in 2026-27 (and what is not)

The CBSE Science curriculum for Class X (2026-27) lists five units. The board paper is 80 marks and internal assessment is 20 marks, for a total of 100.

That is 13 chapters for the board paper. Just as important is what the curriculum says will be assessed only formatively (in school, not in the year-end board paper):

  • Periodic Classification of Elements – Döbereiner, Newlands, Mendeléev and the modern periodic table.
  • Evolution – acquired and inherited traits, speciation, fossils, human evolution. Heredity itself (Mendel, sex determination) stays in the board syllabus.
  • Electric motor, electromagnetic induction and the electric generator from Magnetic Effects of Electric Current.
  • Sources of Energy and Management of Natural Resources are not part of the current Class X course structure at all.
  • Boxed information in the NCERT textbook is not assessed in the year-end exam, according to the curriculum's note for teachers.

Two smaller exclusions matter for revision: the pH chapter does not need the logarithm definition, and in the Human Eye chapter the colour of the sun at sunrise and sunset is excluded. Mirror and lens formulae are to be used, not derived.

If an old guide or a senior's notes tell you to prioritise "Sources of Energy" or "Periodic Classification" for the board paper, they are out of date. Always check the curriculum PDF for your own session on cbseacademic.nic.in before cutting anything.

How the question paper is built

The 2026-27 sample question paper states that there is no change in question paper design or assessment pattern from the previous session. The paper has 39 questions, 80 marks and 3 hours, split into three subject sections.

Question typeNumberMarks eachTotal

Short answer6212
Short answer7321
Case-based / source-based (with sub-parts)3412
Long answer3515

In the sample paper, Section A is Biology (30 marks, which covers World of Living plus Our Environment), Section B is Chemistry (25 marks) and Section C is Physics (25 marks). Each section has its own case-based question and its own 5-mark question. Internal choice is given in roughly a third of the paper.

The curriculum's question paper design weights competencies as follows: 50% demonstrate knowledge and understanding, 30% application of knowledge and concepts, and 20% formulate, analyse, evaluate and create. In practice this means fewer "define X" questions and more "here is an experiment or a situation, explain what happens". The 2026-27 sample paper, for example, asks about lime water in four set-ups kept in sunlight, a mustard flower with its anthers removed, and a solenoid-based door lock. None of these is in the textbook word for word, but every one is answerable from NCERT concepts.

Two practical conclusions follow. First, reading alone will not prepare you; you have to practise applying concepts to unfamiliar set-ups. Second, since each subject section sits together, you can plan your time by section: roughly 60 minutes for Biology, 50 minutes each for Chemistry and Physics, and 15–20 minutes to revise.

The two board exams from 2026

CBSE has moved Class 10 to a two-exam system from the 2026 examinations. The first exam, held from mid-February, is compulsory for everyone. The second, held in May, is optional and is meant for improving performance in up to three subjects from Science, Mathematics, Social Science and the languages. Internal assessment is done only once in the year. Where a student takes both, the better performance counts.

For the 2027 exams, the exact dates will come in CBSE's date sheet, so rely on cbse.gov.in rather than forwarded messages. My advice to students is simple: prepare as if February is the only attempt. The May exam is a safety net, not a plan. It falls in a short window after the first results, when you would also be starting Class 11, and treating it as your real attempt usually means two half-prepared exams instead of one well-prepared one. If Science goes badly in February, though, the second exam is a genuine second chance, and the notes below work for both.

Chemistry notes: Chemical Substances (25 marks)

Chemistry questions reward exact equations with state symbols, correct names of products and observations (colour change, gas evolved, precipitate formed). Learn reactions in groups, not as a list of 60 separate lines.

Chemical Reactions and Equations

  • Signs of a reaction: change in state, colour, evolution of gas, change in temperature, formation of precipitate.
  • Combination: CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + heat (quicklime to slaked lime; exothermic). Also 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (burns with dazzling white flame).
  • Decomposition: thermal – CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; 2FeSO₄ → Fe₂O₃ + SO₂ + SO₃ (green crystals turn brown-red, smell of burning sulphur); 2Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂ (brown fumes). Electrolytic – 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (hydrogen volume is double). Photolytic – 2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂ in sunlight (white turns grey; used in black-and-white photography).
  • Displacement: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (blue solution turns pale green; brown coating on nail).
  • Double displacement / precipitation: Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ (white) + 2NaCl.
  • Oxidation and reduction: gain of oxygen/loss of hydrogen is oxidation; the reverse is reduction. CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O: CuO is reduced, H₂ is oxidised.
  • Everyday effects: corrosion (rusting of iron needs both air and moisture) and rancidity (prevented by antioxidants, nitrogen flushing, airtight containers, refrigeration).

Typical questions: identify the type of reaction, balance and add state symbols, explain an observation, identify the substance oxidised and reduced.

Common error: calling every reaction with heat "decomposition". The quicklime reaction releases heat but is a combination reaction.

Acids, Bases and Salts

  • Definitions for the board: acids give H⁺ (as H₃O⁺) ions in water; bases give OH⁻ ions. Water-soluble bases are alkalis.
  • Indicators: litmus (acid turns blue litmus red), phenolphthalein (pink in base, colourless in acid), methyl orange (red in acid, yellow in base); olfactory indicators such as onion and clove oil lose their smell in a base.
  • Reactions to know: metal + acid → salt + H₂ (Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂; test: pops with a burning splint); metal carbonate/hydrogen carbonate + acid → salt + H₂O + CO₂ (lime water turns milky); acid + base → salt + water (neutralisation); metal oxide + acid → salt + water; Zn + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂.
  • pH: 0–14; below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 basic. Tooth decay starts below 5.5; antacids neutralise excess stomach acid; plants need suitable soil pH; bee sting is treated with a mild base such as baking soda.
  • Chlor-alkali process: 2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g). Chlorine at the anode, hydrogen at the cathode, NaOH near the cathode.
  • Salts to know by formula and use: bleaching powder CaOCl₂ (from Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂); baking soda NaHCO₃ (2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ on heating); washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O; Plaster of Paris CaSO₄·½H₂O, which sets back into gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O with water.
  • Water of crystallisation: blue CuSO₄·5H₂O turns white on heating and blue again when water is added.

Common errors: writing "pH is the log of…" (not required), confusing baking soda with washing soda, and forgetting that dilution of acid is done by adding acid to water, never water to acid.

Metals and Non-metals

The reactivity series is the backbone of this chapter. Learn it top to bottom:

ReactivityMetalsExtraction method
HighK, Na, Ca, Mg, AlElectrolysis of molten chlorides/oxides
MiddleZn, Fe, PbRoasting (sulphide ores) or calcination (carbonate ores), then reduction with carbon
LowCu, Hg, Ag, AuHeating alone (e.g. 2HgS + 3O₂ → 2HgO + 2SO₂, then 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂); Au and Ag occur free
  • Physical properties and exceptions: mercury is a liquid metal; sodium and potassium are soft; graphite (a non-metal) conducts electricity; iodine is lustrous; diamond is very hard.
  • Reactions: metals with oxygen (amphoteric oxides Al₂O₃ and ZnO react with both acids and bases), with water (Na and K violently in cold water; Mg with hot water; Fe with steam: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂), with acids (hydrogen is not usually evolved with nitric acid, except with Mg and Mn and very dilute acid).
  • Ionic compounds: electron-dot formation of NaCl and MgCl₂; high melting points, conduct when molten or in solution, generally soluble in water.
  • Thermite reaction: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ + heat (joining railway tracks).
  • Refining: electrolytic refining of copper – impure copper is the anode, pure copper the cathode, acidified copper sulphate the electrolyte; impurities collect as anode mud.
  • Corrosion: silver turns black (silver sulphide), copper gets a green coat (basic copper carbonate), iron rusts. Prevention: painting, oiling, galvanising, chrome plating, alloying.
  • Alloys: brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), solder (Pb + Sn, low melting point), amalgam (any alloy with mercury).

Common error: drawing electron-dot structures with the wrong number of valence electrons, or showing ionic compounds as sharing electrons.

Carbon and its Compounds

  • Covalent bonding: electron-dot structures of H₂, O₂ (double bond), N₂ (triple bond), CH₄, H₂O and NH₃. Covalent compounds have low melting points and are poor conductors.
  • Versatile nature: catenation and tetravalency; allotropes diamond, graphite and fullerene.
  • Homologous series: consecutive members differ by CH₂ (14 u); similar chemical properties, gradation in physical properties.
  • Nomenclature: prefix by carbon count (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-); suffix -ane, -ene, -yne; functional groups: chloro/bromo (halogen), -ol (alcohol), -al (aldehyde), -one (ketone), -oic acid (carboxylic acid).
  • Reactions: combustion; oxidation of ethanol to ethanoic acid by alkaline KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇; addition (hydrogenation of vegetable oils with nickel catalyst); substitution (CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl in sunlight).
  • Ethanol: 2Na + 2CH₃CH₂OH → 2CH₃CH₂ONa + H₂; with hot concentrated H₂SO₄ at 443 K it dehydrates to ethene.
  • Ethanoic acid: reacts with NaHCO₃ to give CO₂; esterification with ethanol (acid catalyst) gives a sweet-smelling ester; saponification of an ester with NaOH gives soap and alcohol.
  • Soaps and detergents: micelle formation (hydrophobic tail in oil, ionic head in water); soaps form scum with hard water (calcium and magnesium salts), detergents do not.

Common errors: naming CH₃CHO as "ethanol" instead of ethanal, putting five bonds on a carbon in a structure, and forgetting the catalyst in hydrogenation and esterification.

Biology notes: World of Living and Our Environment (30 marks)

Biology is the largest single section in the sample paper. The marks go to precise terms (not "food pipe" when the question expects "oesophagus"), neat labelled diagrams and flow-charts of processes.

Life Processes

  • Nutrition: autotrophic and heterotrophic; photosynthesis 6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O (in the presence of chlorophyll and sunlight). Three events: absorption of light, splitting of water, reduction of CO₂. Stomata open and close by guard cells.
  • Human digestion: mouth (salivary amylase: starch to sugar), stomach (HCl, pepsin, mucus), small intestine (bile emulsifies fats; pancreatic juice has trypsin, lipase, amylase; villi absorb), large intestine (water absorption).
  • Respiration pathways: glucose → pyruvate in the cytoplasm; then ethanol + CO₂ (yeast, no oxygen), lactic acid (muscles, lack of oxygen, causes cramps) or CO₂ + H₂O (mitochondria, with oxygen, most energy). Energy is stored as ATP.
  • Human respiratory system: nostrils, pharynx, larynx, trachea (rings of cartilage), bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli (large surface area for gas exchange). Haemoglobin carries oxygen.
  • Transport in humans: four-chambered heart and double circulation (oxygenated and deoxygenated blood kept separate); arteries (thick walls), veins (valves), capillaries (exchange); platelets for clotting; lymph.
  • Transport in plants: xylem carries water and minerals (transpiration pull), phloem carries food (translocation, needs energy from ATP).
  • Excretion: nephron – glomerulus and Bowman's capsule filter; useful substances such as glucose, amino acids, salts and water are reabsorbed; urine is stored in the bladder. Artificial kidney (dialysis). Plants lose waste through leaves falling, resins, gums and stored in vacuoles.

Must-draw diagrams: stomata (open and closed), human digestive system, human respiratory system, sectional view of the heart, nephron, human excretory system.

Control and Coordination

  • Neuron: dendrite, cell body, axon, nerve ending; synapse carries the signal chemically.
  • Reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay) → motor neuron → effector. Reflexes are fast because the brain is not involved in the immediate response.
  • Brain: fore-brain (thinking, voluntary actions), mid-brain, hind-brain – cerebellum (balance and posture), medulla (involuntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation, vomiting), pons.
  • Plant hormones: auxin (cell elongation, phototropism), gibberellin (stem growth), cytokinin (cell division), abscisic acid (inhibits growth, wilting of leaves).
  • Movements in plants: touch-me-not (Mimosa) responds by changing water content in cells, not by growth; tropisms (photo-, geo-, hydro-, chemo-) are growth movements.
  • Endocrine glands: pituitary (growth hormone; dwarfism/gigantism), thyroid (thyroxine; needs iodine, deficiency causes goitre), pancreas (insulin; diabetes), adrenal (adrenaline; fight-or-flight), testes (testosterone), ovaries (oestrogen). Feedback mechanisms regulate timing and amount.

Common error: listing the pollen tube or the Mimosa response as phototropism. Read the stimulus carefully before naming the tropism.

How do Organisms Reproduce?

  • Why variation matters: DNA copying is not perfectly accurate; variation helps populations survive changing conditions.
  • Asexual modes: binary fission (Amoeba, Leishmania), multiple fission (Plasmodium), fragmentation (Spirogyra), regeneration (Planaria, Hydra), budding (Hydra, yeast), vegetative propagation (Bryophyllum leaves, layering, grafting), spore formation (Rhizopus).
  • Flowering plants: parts of a flower, bisexual and unisexual flowers, self- and cross-pollination, pollen tube growth, fertilisation, zygote → embryo, ovule → seed, ovary → fruit.
  • Humans: changes at puberty; male system (testes, vas deferens, seminal vesicle, prostate); female system (ovaries, oviduct, uterus); fertilisation in the oviduct; placenta for nutrition and waste removal.
  • Reproductive health: contraception – barrier, hormonal, copper-T, surgical (vasectomy blocks the vas deferens, tubectomy blocks the oviduct); sexually transmitted infections; why sex-selective abortion is illegal and harmful.

Must-draw diagrams: binary fission, budding in Hydra, longitudinal section of a flower, pollen tube growth, germination (plumule, radicle, cotyledon), human male and female reproductive systems.

Heredity

  • Key terms: gene, allele, dominant, recessive, genotype, phenotype, homozygous (TT, tt), heterozygous (Tt).
  • Monohybrid cross: tall (TT) × short (tt) → F1 all tall (Tt); F2 phenotype 3 tall : 1 short; genotype 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.
  • Dihybrid cross: round yellow (RRYY) × wrinkled green (rryy) → F1 all round yellow; F2 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, showing that traits are inherited independently.
  • Sex determination in humans: females XX, males XY; the child's sex depends on whether the sperm carries X or Y. In some animals, temperature decides sex.

Typical question: the 2026-27 sample paper gives a cross such as VV × vv and VVTT × vvtt and asks for percentages in F1 and F2. Show the Punnett square, state the ratio and then convert it to a percentage. A trap option asks about "medium height" or "light violet" plants: in Mendel's pea traits one allele is fully dominant, so the answer is 0%, with that reason.

Our Environment

  • Ecosystem: biotic and abiotic components; producers, consumers, decomposers.
  • Food chains and webs: trophic levels; about 1% of sunlight falling on leaves is captured by producers, and roughly 10% of energy passes to the next trophic level, which is why food chains are short.
  • Biological magnification: non-biodegradable chemicals such as pesticides accumulate and are highest at the top trophic level (humans).
  • Ozone: O₂ splits into O atoms under UV, which combine with O₂ to form O₃; ozone in the upper atmosphere shields us from UV; CFCs deplete it.
  • Waste: biodegradable and non-biodegradable; segregation, composting, reuse, recycling.

This is the lightest chapter (5 marks) and one of the easiest to score in full. Do not skip it in the last month.

Physics notes: Natural Phenomena and Effects of Current (25 marks)

Physics marks come from three habits: stating the formula, applying the sign convention or unit correctly, and drawing ray diagrams and circuit diagrams with arrows and labels.

Light – Reflection and Refraction

  • Spherical mirrors: pole, centre of curvature, principal axis, focus; R = 2f.
  • Mirror formula: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f; magnification m = h′/h = −v/u.
  • Refraction: laws of refraction; Snell's law sin i / sin r = constant; refractive index n = c/v. Light bends towards the normal in a denser medium.
  • Lens formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f; m = h′/h = v/u.
  • Power: P = 1/f with f in metres; unit dioptre (D). Convex lens power is positive, concave lens power is negative.
  • Uses: concave mirror – torches, shaving mirrors, dentists; convex mirror – rear-view mirrors (wider field of view, always erect image).

Must-draw ray diagrams: image formation by a concave mirror for all six object positions, by a convex lens for all six positions, and one case each for a convex mirror and a concave lens; refraction through a glass slab showing lateral displacement.

The Human Eye and the Colourful World

  • Parts: cornea (most refraction), iris, pupil, eye lens, ciliary muscles, retina, optic nerve.
  • Power of accommodation: ciliary muscles change the focal length of the lens. Near point about 25 cm for a normal eye; far point at infinity.
  • Defects: myopia (image in front of the retina; concave lens), hypermetropia (image behind the retina; convex lens), presbyopia (bifocal lenses). Causes: eyeball too long/short, or focal length too short/long.
  • Prism: refraction at two surfaces and angle of deviation; dispersion of white light into VIBGYOR (violet deviates most, red least); Newton's second prism recombines the colours.
  • Rainbow: dispersion, refraction and internal reflection in water droplets; seen opposite the sun.
  • Atmospheric refraction: twinkling of stars, planets do not twinkle, advanced sunrise and delayed sunset.
  • Scattering: Tyndall effect; blue sky; danger signals are red because red light is scattered least.

Must-draw diagrams: the human eye, correction of myopia and hypermetropia, refraction through a prism with angles labelled, dispersion.

Electricity

  • Current: I = Q/t; 1 A = 1 C/s. Charge on an electron is 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • Potential difference: V = W/Q; measured by a voltmeter in parallel; ammeter in series.
  • Ohm's law: V = IR at constant temperature; V–I graph is a straight line through the origin; slope gives R.
  • Resistance: R = ρL/A; depends on length, area of cross-section, material and temperature. Resistivity depends only on the material (and temperature).
  • Combinations: series R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ (same current); parallel 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ (same voltage). Household appliances are connected in parallel.
  • Heating effect: H = I²Rt (Joule's law); applications: heater, iron, bulb filament (tungsten), fuse.
  • Power: P = VI = I²R = V²/R; 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

Magnetic Effects of Electric Current

  • Field lines: go from north to south outside a magnet, never intersect, and are closer where the field is stronger.
  • Straight conductor: concentric circles; direction by the right-hand thumb rule; strength increases with current and decreases with distance.
  • Circular loop and solenoid: a solenoid's field inside is uniform and resembles a bar magnet; with a soft iron core it becomes an electromagnet.
  • Force on a conductor: maximum when the current is perpendicular to the field; direction by Fleming's left-hand rule (First finger Field, seCond finger Current, thuMb Motion).
  • AC and DC: AC changes direction periodically; in India the supply is AC at 50 Hz. AC can be transmitted over long distances with less loss.
  • Domestic circuits: live, neutral and earth wires (red, black and green insulation in NCERT); the earth wire protects from shock in metal-bodied appliances; fuse and overloading/short-circuiting.

The motor, electromagnetic induction and the generator are assessed only in school this session, so the board paper will not ask Fleming's right-hand rule or the generator diagram. The sample paper's solenoid door-lock case question shows the style you should expect instead.

Skills that decide your marks

Knowing the chapter is half the job. These five skills convert knowledge into marks, and each one can be practised separately.

Balancing chemical equations

  1. Write the correct formula of every reactant and product first. Never change a subscript to balance.
  2. Start with the compound that has the most atoms; balance metals, then non-metals, then hydrogen, and oxygen last.
  3. Treat a polyatomic ion (SO₄, NO₃, CO₃) as one unit if it appears unchanged on both sides.
  4. Add state symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq) and conditions (heat, sunlight, catalyst) above the arrow.
  5. Count every atom on both sides once more before moving on.

Example: Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂. Balance Fe: 3Fe. Balance O: 4H₂O. Balance H: 4H₂. Final: 3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g).

Drawing labelled diagrams that score

  • Use a sharp pencil, draw about a third of a page, and draw label lines with a ruler that do not cross.
  • Label only what is asked, plus the parts needed to explain it; a clean diagram with six correct labels beats a cluttered one with twelve.
  • Write a title under the diagram ("Sectional view of the human heart").
  • For ray diagrams: arrows on every ray, dotted lines for virtual rays, and the principal axis, F and C (or 2F) marked.
  • For circuits: standard symbols, ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel, and the direction of conventional current.
  • Practise each must-draw diagram at least five times before January. Speed comes from repetition.

Numericals in Light, step by step

Question: an object is placed 20 cm in front of a convex lens of focal length 15 cm. Find the image position and nature.

  1. Write the data with signs: u = −20 cm, f = +15 cm.
  2. Write the formula: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f.
  3. Substitute: 1/v = 1/15 + 1/(−20) = 1/15 − 1/20 = (4 − 3)/60 = 1/60.
  4. Solve: v = +60 cm.
  5. Magnification: m = v/u = 60/(−20) = −3.
  6. Interpret: image is 60 cm behind the lens, real, inverted and three times enlarged.

The interpretation line is where most students drop a mark. Positive v for a lens means real; negative m means inverted.

Numericals in Electricity, step by step

Question: resistors of 4 Ω and 12 Ω are connected in parallel, and this combination is in series with a 3 Ω resistor across a 12 V battery. Find the total current and the current through the 4 Ω resistor.

  1. Parallel part: 1/Rp = 1/4 + 1/12 = 3/12 + 1/12 = 4/12, so Rp = 3 Ω.
  2. Total resistance: R = 3 + 3 = 6 Ω.
  3. Total current: I = V/R = 12/6 = 2 A.
  4. Voltage across the parallel part: V = IRp = 2 × 3 = 6 V.
  5. Current through 4 Ω: I = 6/4 = 1.5 A (and 0.5 A through 12 Ω; the two add up to 2 A, which is your check).

Always draw the circuit first, even if the question does not ask for it, and write the unit on every line of the answer.

Writing 3- and 5-mark answers

  • Match points to marks: a 3-mark answer needs three distinct, correct points; a 5-mark answer usually needs a diagram or equation plus four or five points.
  • Use the verb: "state" wants a line; "explain" wants cause and effect; "distinguish" wants a table with at least three points of difference.
  • Keywords first: start with the exact term (double circulation, biological magnification, lateral displacement) and then explain it.
  • Equations and diagrams earn marks on their own: in Chemistry and Biology, a correct equation or labelled diagram often carries a mark even if the text is brief.
  • Case-based questions: read the passage once for the idea and once for the data; answer each sub-part separately and label them (A, B, C).

Practicals and internal assessment (20 marks)

Internal assessment is 20 marks: periodic assessment (5), multiple assessment (5), portfolio (5) and subject enrichment through practical work (5). The curriculum lists 14 experiments, meant to be done alongside theory, and experiment-based questions also appear in the board paper itself.

  • Finding the pH of dilute HCl, dilute NaOH, dilute ethanoic acid, lemon juice, water and dilute hydrogen carbonate solution; properties of HCl and NaOH with litmus, zinc and sodium carbonate.
  • Classifying reactions: water on quicklime, heating ferrous sulphate, iron nails in copper sulphate, sodium sulphate with barium chloride.
  • Action of Zn, Fe, Cu and Al on their salt solutions, and arranging them by reactivity.
  • V–I graph for a resistor (Ohm's law); equivalent resistance of resistors in series and parallel.
  • Temporary mount of a leaf peel to show stomata; showing CO₂ is released during respiration.
  • Properties of acetic acid; cleaning capacity of soap in soft and hard water.
  • Focal length of a concave mirror and a convex lens; path of light through a glass slab and a prism.
  • Binary fission in Amoeba and budding in yeast and Hydra from slides; parts of a dicot embryo (pea, gram or red kidney bean).

For each experiment, know the aim, the key observation, the precaution that matters most and the one error that ruins the result. For example, in the focal length experiment the object must be distant; in the glass slab experiment the angle of emergence equals the angle of incidence; in the soap experiment hard water gives less foam and more scum. The portfolio is also where the formative-only topics (periodic classification, evolution, motor and generator) may be credited, so do those write-ups properly rather than copying them.

The last 30 days before the exam

Day 30–25: read NCERT line by line for all Chemistry chapters; rewrite the reaction sheet.

Day 24–20: NCERT line by line for Biology; draw every must-draw diagram once.

Day 19–15: Physics formula sheet; 20 mixed numericals; sign convention table from memory.

Day 14: full sample paper under exam conditions, 3 hours, no phone.

Day 13–11: fix every mistake from Day 14; redo weak chapters' NCERT exercises.

Day 10: second full paper; mark it strictly with the marking scheme.

Day 9–7: case-based and assertion–reason questions from recent CBSE papers.

Day 6: third full paper; time each section separately.

Day 5–4: revise the error log only; no new books or new question banks.

Day 3–2: reaction sheet, diagram sheet, formula sheet, one reading each.

Day 1: light revision of definitions; pack admit card, pens, pencil, ruler, eraser.

Exam morning: no new topics; read the paper fully in the reading time first.

24 common mistakes that cost marks

Unbalanced equations – a correct reaction written unbalanced usually loses part of the mark. Balance every equation, every time.

Missing state symbols – when the question says "balanced chemical equation with state symbols", (s), (aq) and (g) carry marks.

Changing subscripts to balance – writing H₂O₂ to fix oxygen changes the substance. Change coefficients only.

Confusing baking and washing soda – NaHCO₃ and Na₂CO₃·10H₂O have different uses; mixing them up is a frequent slip.

Wrong Plaster of Paris formula – it is CaSO₄·½H₂O; gypsum is CaSO₄·2H₂O.

Reactivity series gaps – forgetting where Al, Zn and Pb sit makes displacement questions guesswork.

Ionic bonds drawn as shared pairs – NaCl and MgCl₂ involve transfer of electrons; show the charges.

Nomenclature slips – ethanal is an aldehyde, ethanol an alcohol, ethanoic acid a carboxylic acid.

Vague biology terms – "food pipe" and "air bags" instead of oesophagus and alveoli will not earn full marks.

Unlabelled or over-labelled diagrams – label what is asked, with ruled lines and a title.

Heart diagram oriented wrong – the left side of the heart is on the right of your drawing; label it that way.

Aerobic vs anaerobic muddle – say where each step happens (cytoplasm or mitochondria) and name the products.

Tropism names guessed – identify the stimulus first, then the tropism.

Punnett squares skipped – ratios without a cross rarely get full marks in heredity questions.

Ignoring sex determination basics – the father's sperm (X or Y) decides the child's sex; state it clearly.

Sign convention errors – u is negative for a real object; f is negative for a concave mirror and a concave lens.

No interpretation of v and m – state real/virtual, erect/inverted and size after every Light numerical.

Power in centimetres – P = 1/f needs f in metres before you calculate dioptres.

Missing arrows in ray diagrams – rays without arrows and virtual rays drawn solid lose marks.

Units dropped – Ω, A, V, J, W and kWh must appear in the final answer.

kWh treated as power – it is energy; convert with 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

Ammeter and voltmeter swapped – ammeter in series, voltmeter in parallel, in both circuits and practicals.

Studying excluded topics – time spent on generators or periodic tables for the board paper is time taken from Carbon or Heredity.

No timed papers – many students know the content but finish the Physics section in a hurry. Time yourself from December.

When a science home tutor helps

Many students manage Class 10 Science with school teaching and steady self-study. A tutor is worth considering when a specific problem is not going away on its own:

  • Numericals in Light or Electricity are consistently wrong, and you cannot see where.
  • Chemistry feels like memorising random equations rather than a pattern.
  • Half-yearly or pre-board scores are well below what the student understands in conversation – usually an answer-writing problem.
  • One section (often Physics or Chemistry) drags the whole paper down.
  • The student needs structure and someone to check work weekly, especially from October onwards.
  • A family is planning for the May improvement exam and wants focused, short-term help.

A good science tutor does not re-teach the whole book. They diagnose where marks are lost, fix the underlying concept, and then drill answer-writing, diagrams and numericals until they are automatic. One tutor or subject specialists?

For most Class 10 students, one good science tutor covering all three sections works well, because the board paper is a single subject and the tutor can balance time across Physics, Chemistry and Biology. Subject specialists make sense when the gap is concentrated: a student who is strong in Biology but lost in Physics numericals may benefit more from a Physics-strong tutor for a few months. Students also preparing for competitive exams later sometimes prefer a tutor who teaches with that foundation in mind; if Biology and NEET are on the horizon, our guide on NCERT-first NEET Biology explains why the Class 10 base matters.

How to choose a tutor: demo checklist

Ask the tutor to teach a topic the student finds hard, not an easy one.

Check that they use the 2026-27 syllabus and know which topics are formative only.

Watch whether they ask the student questions or only lecture.

See if they correct answer-writing: keywords, units, diagram labels.

Ask how they will track progress – weekly tests, error log, timed papers.

Ask the student afterwards whether the explanation made sense.

Agree the schedule and goals for the next two months before starting.

Frequently asked questions

Is NCERT enough for Class 10 Science?

For the board paper, NCERT is the foundation and covers every concept that can be asked. Add NCERT Exemplar for harder application questions and CBSE sample papers for the paper pattern. Extra reference books help only after NCERT is done thoroughly.

Which chapters carry the most marks?

By unit, Chemical Substances and World of Living carry 25 marks each; Effects of Current 13, Natural Phenomena 12 and Our Environment 5. Chapter-wise marks vary from paper to paper, so do not skip any examined chapter.

Are Periodic Classification and Evolution in the board exam?

The 2026-27 curriculum says these topics, along with the motor, electromagnetic induction and generator, are assessed only formatively in school and not in the year-end board exam. Check your own session's curriculum before deciding.

How many hours a day should I study Science?

Consistency matters more than hours. Around an hour a day through the year, with more time during revision months, is enough for most students if the time is spent solving and writing rather than rereading.

Should I plan to use the May exam?

Prepare as though February is your only attempt. The May exam is useful if something goes wrong, but it comes in a short window and is best treated as a backup.
