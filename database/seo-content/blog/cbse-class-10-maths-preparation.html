Class 10 maths is the one board paper where a plan pays off most. The syllabus is fixed, the textbook is fixed, the question paper design has not changed for 2026-27, and the marking scheme rewards clear working step by step. Students who lose marks usually understand the chapters. They lose marks through careless algebra, missing steps, weak revision of the lighter chapters, and too little practice under time pressure.

This guide is written for the 2026-27 batch, who sit the board exam in 2027. It covers the official syllabus and weightage, the paper pattern, the choice between Standard and Basic, how the two board exams work, a chapter-by-chapter guide to all 14 NCERT chapters, a month-by-month plan, a last-30-days plan, and the mistakes that cost marks year after year. Read it once from start to finish, then come back to the section you need.

CBSE Class 10 Maths 2026-27 at a glance

Syllabus – 14 NCERT chapters in seven units. The current NCERT Class 10 textbook is the prescribed book.

Marks – 80 for the theory paper and 20 for internal assessment, 100 in total.

Paper – 3 hours, 38 compulsory questions in five sections (A to E), with internal choice in some questions.

Two levels – Mathematics Standard (code 041) and Mathematics Basic (code 241). Both use the same syllabus and textbook.

Two board exams – the first exam in February is compulsory. The second exam in May is optional and is meant for improving your score.

No calculators – calculators are not allowed, and you take π = 22/7 unless the question says otherwise.

The CBSE sample paper for 2026-27 says plainly that there is no change in the question paper design and assessment pattern for this session. So everything in the 2025-26 pattern still applies, and the recent sample papers and previous board papers remain the right practice material.

Syllabus and unit-wise weightage

Three things stand out. Algebra alone is a quarter of the theory paper, so the four algebra chapters deserve the most practice. Geometry and Trigonometry together carry 27 marks, and this is where proofs and diagrams decide your score. And Statistics and Probability together give 11 marks for fairly predictable work, which makes them some of the easiest marks in the paper to secure.

What is not in the syllabus any more

Many older guides, guidebooks and even some school notes still carry topics that the current syllabus does not include. Constructions is no longer a chapter (older lists that show "Constructions" as chapter 11 are out of date). The current syllabus also has no Euclid's division lemma, no frustum of a cone, no area of a triangle from coordinates, and no complementary-angle trigonometric ratios. If a practice book is full of these, it was written for an older syllabus. Check the date on any material before you spend hours on it.

Limits the syllabus sets, and why they matter

The official syllabus also puts limits on several topics. Knowing them saves time and tells you what the examiner can and cannot ask:

  • Heights and distances problems should not involve more than two right triangles, and the angles of elevation or depression should be only 30°, 45° or 60°.
  • For the area of a segment of a circle, the central angle is restricted to 60°, 90° and 120°.
  • Surface areas and volumes cover combinations of any two of these solids: cubes, cuboids, spheres, hemispheres and right circular cylinders or cones.
  • Statistics covers the mean, median and mode of grouped data, and bimodal situations are avoided.
  • Quadratic equations cover only real roots, found by factorisation or the quadratic formula.
  • Trigonometric identities are limited to simple identities built on sin²A + cos²A = 1.
  • Coordinate geometry covers the distance formula and the section formula for internal division.

How the paper tests different skills

The CBSE curriculum also gives a design for the kinds of thinking the paper tests. This is where Standard and Basic differ most:

Type of questionStandard (041)Basic (241)
Remembering and understanding43 marks (about 54%)60 marks (about 75%)
Applying19 marks (about 24%)12 marks (about 15%)
Analysing, evaluating and creating18 marks (about 22%)8 marks (about 10%)

In practice, this means close to half of a Standard paper asks you to apply a method in a new setting or reason through something unfamiliar. Learning the NCERT exercises by heart will not be enough for those marks. You need to understand why each method works, so you can recognise it when the question is dressed up as a story about a park, a kite or a water tank. Case studies and assertion–reason questions are the most obvious examples, but the application questions are spread through every section.

How to handle the question types

MCQs – Do the working in the margin or rough column. Many options are chosen to match a common slip (a wrong sign, a forgotten square root). Check your answer quickly by substituting it back.

Assertion–reason – Judge each statement on its own first. Only then ask whether the reason actually explains the assertion. Most errors come from skipping that last step.

2-mark questions – Keep them short but complete: formula, substitution, answer with unit. The ½-mark splits in the marking scheme reward each step.

3-mark questions – Irrationality proofs, zeros of polynomials, AP word problems and short trigonometry proofs often land here. Write the reasoning, not just the working.

5-mark questions – Theorem proofs, heights and distances, quadratic word problems, statistics tables and solid-combination problems. Draw the figure, state what is given, and set out every step.

Case studies – Read the passage once for the story and once for the numbers. The 1-mark parts are usually direct. The 2-mark part carries a choice, so read both options before you pick.

Standard vs Basic maths: which should you choose?

Both papers are set on the same 14 chapters and the same NCERT textbook, carry the same 80 + 20 marks, and follow the same five-section layout. The difference is in how the questions are pitched. As the table above shows, about three-quarters of the Basic paper tests recall and understanding, against about half of the Standard paper. Standard has more multi-step application and reasoning questions, and its long answers tend to demand more.

Choose Standard if

You want to take Mathematics in Class 11 and 12. This covers the science stream with maths (PCM), engineering entrance preparation, commerce students who want core maths, and anyone aiming at economics, data or finance degrees where maths is expected.

Choose Standard also if you are undecided about Class 11 but are doing reasonably well in maths now. It keeps every option open, and a sound Class 9 foundation usually makes the Standard paper manageable with regular practice.

Choose Basic if

You are fairly sure you will not take mathematics in Class 11 (for example, humanities without maths), and maths is currently causing you real stress or pulling your overall percentage down. Basic lets you secure a good score on the same content with fewer heavy application questions.

What the choice means for Class 11

Traditionally, CBSE allowed Basic students to take only Applied Mathematics in Class 11, not core Mathematics. In May 2025, CBSE relaxed this rule. Schools may now allow a student who passed Basic to take Mathematics in Class 11 if the school is satisfied the student can manage it. The rule may change again as new curricula come in, and each school decides for its own students. Ask your school what its current policy is before you register.

Even where the switch is allowed, the jump from Basic to Class 11 Mathematics is real. Class 11 builds on algebraic manipulation, trigonometry and coordinate geometry at the level the Standard paper expects. A student who chose Basic and then takes Class 11 Maths should plan to close that gap in the summer holidays.

Your school registers your choice with CBSE when it submits the list of candidates, usually early in Class 10. Decide before then, and talk it through with your maths teacher. Their view of your work counts for more than one bad unit test.

The two board exams: how to use them well

From 2026, CBSE holds two board exams for Class 10, following NEP 2020. Here is how the scheme works, as announced by CBSE and reported in education news:

  • The first exam, held in February, is compulsory for all students. Its results come out in April.
  • The second exam, held in May, is optional. It is meant for students who want to improve their performance, and its results come out in June.
  • In the second exam, a student can try to improve up to three subjects from Science, Mathematics, Social Science and the languages.
  • Internal assessment is carried out only once in the session. Your 20 internal marks carry over and cannot be improved by the second exam.
  • Both exams follow the full syllabus.

In the first cycle (2026), the main exams began on 17 February and the second exams began on 15 May. CBSE has not yet published the 2027 date sheet. Expect a similar calendar, but take the exact dates from cbse.gov.in when they are announced.

My advice to students is simple: prepare as if the February exam is the only exam. The second exam is a safety net, not a plan. Here is why:

The syllabus is the same – You gain nothing by waiting. The second exam tests the same 14 chapters.

April is Class 11 time – If you rely on the May exam, you are revising Class 10 maths while Class 11 has already started.

Three subjects only – You may want that second chance for science or social science, not maths.

The internal 20 is fixed – Periodic tests, portfolio and lab work across the year are the only way to earn those marks.

If you do decide to take maths again in May, treat the April results as a diagnosis. Look at which sections lost marks, go back to the chapter guide below for those chapters, and work through two or three full timed papers each week until the exam.

Internal assessment: the 20 marks you earn during the year

The 2026-27 curriculum splits the 20 internal marks the same way for Standard and Basic:

Portfolio5

Total20

Schools run these differently, so ask your teacher how your school counts periodic tests and what it wants in the portfolio. A few habits help everywhere:

Treat every periodic test as a mini board exam, with full steps.

Keep your maths notebook complete and neat; it often forms part of the portfolio.

Do the lab activities yourself and record the observations properly.

Use the lab activities to understand results you will use later, such as the BPT or tangent lengths.

Submit work on time; late or missing work is the easiest way to lose internal marks.

A student who scores well in internal assessment starts the board exam with most of 20 marks already secured. That is a real buffer. Do not treat it as a formality.

Chapter-by-chapter guide to all 14 chapters

For each chapter: what the board tests, the questions you should expect, where students go wrong, and one tip that raises your score. The order follows the NCERT textbook.

1. Real Numbers

What is tested: The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (prime factorisation), HCF and LCM through prime factors, the relation HCF × LCM = product of two numbers, and proofs that numbers like √2, √3, √5 and 3 + 2√5 are irrational.

Typical questions: MCQs on HCF and LCM; 2-mark word problems (bells ringing together, the longest ruler that measures several lengths exactly); a 3-mark irrationality proof.

Where students go wrong: They use HCF × LCM = product for three numbers, where it does not hold. They pick HCF when the problem needs LCM (and the reverse). In proofs, they forget to say that a and b are co-prime, or they end without naming the contradiction.

Score-maximising tip: Learn one irrationality proof template thoroughly and write it the same way every time. The ½-marks sit on the assumption, the rearrangement, the contradiction and the conclusion.

2. Polynomials

What is tested: Zeros of a polynomial, reading the number of zeros from a graph, and the relationship between the zeros and coefficients of a quadratic polynomial (α + β = −b/a, αβ = c/a).

Typical questions: Counting zeros from a graph (MCQ); finding a quadratic from the given sum and product of zeros; evaluating expressions such as α² + β² or 1/α + 1/β without finding the zeros.

Where students go wrong: Sign errors in −b/a. Counting the points where the graph touches the y-axis instead of the x-axis. Solving for α and β separately when a symmetric expression would take two lines.

Score-maximising tip: Write α + β and αβ as the first line of every question, then rewrite the asked expression in terms of them. It is faster, and it is where the marking scheme puts the marks.

3. Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

What is tested: The graphical method, consistency and inconsistency, the algebraic conditions for one, none or infinitely many solutions (comparing a₁/a₂, b₁/b₂, c₁/c₂), and solution by substitution and elimination, including simple word problems.

Typical questions: "For what value of k…" MCQs on the ratio conditions; 3-mark or 5-mark word problems (ages, fares, fractions, digits); occasionally a graphical solution with the area of the triangle formed with an axis.

Where students go wrong: Mixing up the conditions for "no solution" and "infinitely many solutions". Defining the variables vaguely in word problems. Arithmetic slips during elimination.

Score-maximising tip: In every word problem, open with "Let the … be x and the … be y" in full words. It earns the first step mark and stops you solving for the wrong quantity.

4. Quadratic Equations

What is tested: The standard form ax² + bx + c = 0, solving by factorisation and the quadratic formula (real roots only), the discriminant and the nature of roots, and word problems from daily life.

Typical questions: Nature-of-roots MCQs and "find k for equal roots"; solving by formula (3 marks); word problems on speed and time, area, ages, or work done (often 5 marks).

Where students go wrong: Rewriting the equation into standard form incorrectly before using the formula. Errors with b² when b is negative. Keeping a negative root for a length or speed without comment.

Score-maximising tip: When a root is rejected, write one line saying why ("speed cannot be negative, so x = 45"). That sentence is often a marked step.

5. Arithmetic Progressions

What is tested: Deriving and using the nth term aₙ = a + (n − 1)d and the sum Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n − 1)d], plus real-life applications.

Typical questions: The middle term or nth term from the end (MCQ); the sum of multiples of a number within a range; instalments, savings or seating rows as word problems; polygon angles in AP, as in the 2026-27 sample paper.

Where students go wrong: Counting terms incorrectly (n is off by one). Using Sₙ when the question asks for a single term. Forgetting to check that n is a positive whole number.

Score-maximising tip: Before substituting, write out a, d, n and what is being asked. Most AP errors are errors of reading, not algebra.

6. Triangles

What is tested: Similar figures, the Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT, to be proved), its converse (stated), and the AA, SSS and SAS similarity criteria (stated and applied).

Typical questions: MCQs on ratios of sides and perimeters of similar triangles; 2-mark or 3-mark similarity proofs; a 5-mark question built on the BPT proof or its application.

Where students go wrong: Writing the vertices of similar triangles in the wrong order, which spoils every ratio that follows. Proving similarity without a reason next to each angle. Using congruence criteria when similarity is needed.

Score-maximising tip: Match the vertex order carefully (if ∠A = ∠P, A and P go first in both names). Then every ratio you write is automatically correct.

7. Coordinate Geometry

What is tested: The distance formula and the section formula (internal division), including the midpoint.

Typical questions: Distance and midpoint MCQs; finding the ratio in which a point or an axis divides a segment; showing points form an isosceles or right triangle, a parallelogram or a square; points of trisection.

Where students go wrong: Mixing up m₁ and m₂ in the section formula. Proving a quadrilateral is a square by checking only the sides. Leaving the ratio as a fraction instead of m : n.

Score-maximising tip: For shape proofs, write down what you need before you calculate (for example, four equal sides and equal diagonals for a square). Then compute only those lengths.

8. Introduction to Trigonometry

What is tested: Trigonometric ratios of an acute angle, values at 0°, 30°, 45°, 60° and 90°, relations between the ratios, and simple identities based on sin²A + cos²A = 1.

Typical questions: Evaluating expressions with standard values; finding all ratios from one given ratio; finding A and B from tan(A + B) and tan(A − B); proving an identity (3 or 5 marks).

Where students go wrong: Working on both sides of an identity at once and "meeting in the middle" without clear logic. Writing sin²A as sin A². Forgetting which ratios are undefined at 0° or 90°.

Score-maximising tip: Prove identities by starting from the more complicated side and working only on that side until it becomes the other. Converting to sin and cos is a reliable fallback.

9. Some Applications of Trigonometry

What is tested: Heights and distances using angles of elevation and depression, with at most two right triangles and angles of 30°, 45° or 60° only.

Typical questions: A 5-mark problem with two observation points or a tower on a building; shorter questions on a kite string, a ladder or a shadow; case studies with a real-life scene.

Where students go wrong: Placing the angle of depression inside the triangle incorrectly. Drawing a figure that does not match the words. Not using the fact that the angle of depression equals the angle of elevation (alternate angles).

Score-maximising tip: Draw and label the figure first. It usually carries marks by itself, and a correct figure makes the equations almost write themselves.

10. Circles

What is tested: Tangents, with two theorems to prove: the tangent at any point is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact, and tangents drawn from an external point are equal in length.

Typical questions: MCQs on tangent lengths and angles; a 3-mark or 5-mark proof of either theorem; application questions such as a quadrilateral circumscribing a circle (AB + CD = AD + BC) or the angle between two tangents.

Where students go wrong: Using the theorem they were asked to prove. Missing the "Given, To prove, Construction" structure. Not stating the reason at each step.

Score-maximising tip: Practise writing both proofs from memory on a blank page until they take under five minutes each. They are among the most reliable marks in the paper.

11. Areas Related to Circles

What is tested: Area and arc length of sectors and area of segments (central angle 60°, 90° or 120° only), and perimeter and area problems with these figures.

Typical questions: Sector area from arc length (MCQ); minute-hand or pendulum problems; segment area with a 60° or 90° angle; shaded-region problems combining shapes.

Where students go wrong: Using the full circle's area instead of the sector's. Forgetting to subtract the triangle when finding a segment. Mixing up arc length and area. Leaving square roots unsimplified when the question gives √3 = 1.73.

Score-maximising tip: For segment questions, write "segment = sector − triangle" as a line of working. It shows method even if the arithmetic goes wrong.

12. Surface Areas and Volumes

What is tested: Surface areas and volumes of combinations of any two of cubes, cuboids, spheres, hemispheres, cylinders and cones.

Typical questions: A toy (cone on a hemisphere), a capsule, a tent, a hemispherical bowl, or a cylinder with conical cavities. Often a 5-mark question or a case study.

Where students go wrong: Adding the full surface areas of both solids, including the hidden faces where they join. Confusing curved and total surface area. Using the diameter as the radius. Unit mix-ups (cm² vs cm³, m vs cm).

Score-maximising tip: Before calculating, list the visible surfaces in words ("curved surface of cone + curved surface of hemisphere"). Most lost marks in this chapter come from counting the wrong surfaces.

13. Statistics

What is tested: Mean of grouped data (direct, assumed mean and step-deviation methods), median and mode of grouped data by formula, and the relation between mean, median and mode.

Typical questions: Using Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean (MCQ); finding a missing frequency given the mean or median; a full 5-mark median or mode table.

Where students go wrong: Choosing the wrong median class (cf must first reach n/2). Using f₀ and f₂ from the wrong rows in the mode formula. Not converting inclusive classes (10–19, 20–29) into continuous classes before finding the median.

Score-maximising tip: Draw the full table with neat column headings, and write the formula before substituting. Examiners give step marks for the table, the class chosen and the substitution.

14. Probability

What is tested: The classical definition of probability and simple problems about the likelihood of an event.

Typical questions: Cards (including a pack with some cards removed), two dice, coins, numbered tickets, and "either a perfect square or a perfect cube" types. Mostly 1 to 3 marks.

Where students go wrong: Getting the total number of outcomes wrong (36 for two dice, a reduced total when cards are missing). Double-counting when two conditions overlap. Giving a probability greater than 1 and not noticing.

Score-maximising tip: Write the total outcomes and list the favourable outcomes explicitly. Listing makes double-counting visible, and the marking scheme often rewards the list itself.

How to use NCERT, exemplar, sample papers and previous papers

Good material used in the wrong order wastes time. This is the order I recommend for each chapter, and for revision as a whole:

1. NCERT textbook – Read the chapter, work through the solved examples, and do every exercise question yourself. The board paper is built on this book, and many questions are NCERT ideas with new numbers.

2. NCERT exemplar – Once the textbook exercises are comfortable, move to the exemplar. It is on the prescribed book list and it stretches you on the same syllabus, which is exactly what the application questions need. Standard students should do most of it; Basic students can focus on the short-answer sections.

3. CBSE sample papers and marking schemes – Start the current 2026-27 sample paper once you have covered most of the syllabus. Work through it under timed conditions, then check against the official marking scheme to see where each ½-mark goes.

4. Previous board papers – Use the last few years' papers from CBSE for timed practice. Skip any question on a topic no longer in the syllabus.

5. Your own error log – The last resource, and the most valuable. Every mistake from tests and papers goes in, with the correction and the reason.

A reference book with extra questions is fine for more practice in weak chapters. Check that it follows the current syllabus, and do not let it replace NCERT or the exemplar. The CBSE academic website (cbseacademic.nic.in) publishes the curriculum, sample papers and marking schemes free of charge.

Month-by-month plan: April to the board exam

This plan assumes a school session that starts in April and a first board exam in mid-February, as in 2026. Your school's order of chapters may differ. Keep the principle (learn, practise, revise, test) and adjust the chapter names to match.

April–May: build the base

Real Numbers, Polynomials and Pair of Linear Equations. Finish every NCERT exercise as each chapter is taught, not later. Start an error log in a separate notebook. If you finished Class 9 with gaps in algebra (identities, factorisation, linear equations), close them now. They affect half the Class 10 syllabus.

June (summer break): consolidate and look ahead

Revise the April–May chapters with exemplar questions. Read ahead on Quadratic Equations and Arithmetic Progressions, so that the school lessons become second exposure rather than first. Keep it to about an hour a day; a steady routine matters more than long days.

July–August: algebra and geometry

Quadratic Equations, Arithmetic Progressions, Triangles and Coordinate Geometry. Practise word problems every week, because they are the most common weak spot. Write the BPT proof from memory at least once a week. Prepare properly for your periodic tests, since they count towards internal assessment.

September: trigonometry and mid-term

Introduction to Trigonometry and Applications of Trigonometry. Learn the standard values table so well that you never need to reconstruct it. Around this time, CBSE usually releases the new sample papers (the 2026-27 set came out in September). Download the maths sample paper and marking scheme and keep them for later. Finalise your Standard/Basic decision with your teacher.

October–November: the remaining chapters

Circles, Areas Related to Circles, Surface Areas and Volumes, Statistics and Probability. These are the chapters most often rushed at the end of the school syllabus, so do not leave them. Once the syllabus is complete, begin one full timed paper every two weeks.

December: first full revision

Revise unit by unit in order of weightage: Algebra, Geometry, Trigonometry, Statistics and Probability, Mensuration, then Number Systems and Coordinate Geometry. Solve one full paper a week under exam conditions. Most schools hold pre-board exams in December or January; take them seriously and analyse every lost mark.

January: papers and error log

Two full papers a week, strictly timed at three hours. After each paper, spend an hour on analysis: which marks were lost through concept gaps, which through careless errors, and which through presentation. Update the error log. Finish internal assessment work (portfolio, lab activities) in good time.

February: the final weeks

Follow the last-30-days plan below. After the first board exam, if you plan to take the optional second exam, return to the chapters that cost you marks and repeat the January routine.

The last 30 days: a day-block plan

In the final month, you are not learning new chapters. You are sharpening speed, accuracy and presentation. Split the month into blocks:

Days 30–21: close the gaps

One full timed paper every alternate day.

Error-log review every evening, 20 minutes.

Two weakest chapters: redo their NCERT exercises and exemplar sets.

Rewrite all four proofs (BPT, two circle theorems, one irrationality proof).

Make a one-page formula sheet for each unit, in your own handwriting.

Days 20–11: full papers and speed

One full paper a day, in the same 3-hour slot as the real exam if possible.

Check each paper against a marking scheme, not just an answer key.

Time Section A separately; aim to finish it in about 20–25 minutes.

Practise the case studies from every available sample paper.

Sleep on time; tired practice creates new careless errors.

Days 10–4: revise, do not cram

Alternate days: one full paper, then a day of mixed revision.

Formula sheets and standard trigonometric values every morning.

Go through NCERT solved examples chapter by chapter.

Redo only the questions you got wrong earlier, not the whole book.

No new reference books at this stage.

Days 3–1: calm and ready

Light revision: formula sheets, error log, proofs.

One section of a paper at most, just to stay in rhythm.

Check your admit card, pens, pencil, eraser, ruler and geometry box.

Plan the route and the time you will leave for the centre.

Sleep a full night before the exam; it matters more than one more hour of revision.

On the exam day

Use the reading time to scan the whole paper and mark the questions you are confident about. Many students do Section A first, then the sections in order. Others start with the long answers while they are fresh. Either works if you have practised it in mock papers. Do not experiment on the day. Keep about 10–15 minutes at the end to check that you have attempted every question, including all sub-parts of the case studies.

Step marking and answer presentation

The CBSE marking scheme awards marks for steps, often in ½-mark units. You can see this in the official 2026-27 marking scheme: a 2-mark sector-area question splits its marks between the radius, the formula with substitution, and the final answer. This has two consequences. A right answer with no working can lose marks. And a wrong final answer with correct method can still earn most of the marks.

Here is a short example of how to set out a 3-mark answer:

Solve 2x² − 7x + 3 = 0 using the quadratic formula.

Here a = 2, b = −7, c = 3.

D = b² − 4ac = 49 − 24 = 25 > 0, so the roots are real and distinct.

x = (−b ± √D) / 2a = (7 ± 5) / 4.

x = 12/4 = 3 or x = 2/4 = 1/2.

Hence the roots are 3 and 1/2.

Each line is a step an examiner can give marks for. Writing a, b and c first also prevents the most common error: taking b as 7 instead of −7.

Presentation rules that protect your marks

Write the formula before you substitute into it.

Draw figures in pencil with a ruler, labelled clearly.

Give the reason in brackets beside each geometry step.

Box or underline the final answer, with units.

Keep rough work in a margin column, not mixed into the answer.

Start each section on a fresh page and number every answer exactly as in the paper.

For case studies, write the sub-part number, for example 36 (i), 36 (ii).

If you attempt an internal choice twice, cross out the one you do not want marked.

Common mistakes that cost marks (and the fix for each)

These mistakes come up again and again in Class 10 board answers and mock tests. Almost all of them are fixable with a habit, not extra hours.

Dropping the negative sign of b – Write a, b, c explicitly before using the quadratic formula or zero–coefficient relations.

Squaring a negative carelessly – Put negative numbers in brackets when substituting: (−3)² = 9.

Answers with no working – Even in 2-mark questions, show formula, substitution and result.

No units, or wrong units – Write cm, cm² or cm³ in the final line; check whether the question wants m or cm.

Radius and diameter confused – Underline "diameter" in the question and halve it on the first line.

Hidden surfaces counted – In combined solids, list only the visible surfaces before calculating.

Wrong vertex order in similar triangles – Match equal angles first, then write the names in the same order.

Geometry steps without reasons – Add the reason (alternate angles, BPT, tangent ⊥ radius) beside each line.

Proving with the result you are proving – Only use theorems proved earlier or given in the question.

No figure for heights and distances – Always draw and label the figure; it often carries marks by itself.

Angle of depression put in the wrong place – Measure it from the horizontal at the observer, then use alternate angles.

Identity proved by working both sides – Start from one side and transform it into the other.

Wrong median class – Find n/2 first, then the first class whose cumulative frequency reaches it.

Inclusive classes not adjusted – Convert 10–19, 20–29 to 9.5–19.5, 19.5–29.5 before using the median or mode formula.

Total outcomes miscounted – Write the total first (36 for two dice, 52 minus removed cards).

Overlapping outcomes counted twice – List them; in "square or cube" questions, 64 appears in both lists.

n off by one in AP questions – Find n from aₙ = a + (n − 1)d rather than counting by eye.

No-solution and infinite-solution conditions swapped – Learn them with one example each and test yourself weekly.

Negative root kept for a length or time – Reject it with a one-line reason.

HCF × LCM rule used for three numbers – It only holds for two numbers; for three, use prime factorisation.

Assertion–reason judged together – Check each statement separately, then check whether the reason explains the assertion.

Case-study sub-part left blank – Attempt every part; the 1-mark parts are often direct.

Too long on one question – If a question stalls for more than a few minutes, move on and return later.

Not checking the end of the paper – Keep the last 10–15 minutes to find unattempted questions and obvious slips.

When does a home tutor help?

Most students can prepare for Class 10 maths with school teaching, NCERT and steady practice. A tutor is worth it when something specific is getting in the way. The common cases are:

Weak foundations – Gaps from Class 8 or 9 in algebra or fractions keep turning up as errors in Class 10 chapters.

Marks stuck at one level – The student understands in class but keeps losing marks in tests, and nobody has worked out why.

Word problems and case studies – The student can do the exercise questions but freezes when the same idea is dressed up in a story.

No feedback on written work – Mock papers are done, but nobody marks them against a marking scheme.

Standard or Basic decision – A second opinion helps when the family is unsure whether Standard is manageable.

Second exam – A focused plan between the April results and the May exam, for students who choose to improve their maths score.

What a good Class 10 maths session looks like

A useful session is not a second school lecture. In an hour, a good tutor usually starts by checking the homework and the error log, spends most of the time on problems the student solves while the tutor watches, and ends by setting targeted practice. They explain why a method works, not only the steps. They insist on full written working. And every few weeks they set a timed test and go through it as a CBSE examiner would, ½-mark by ½-mark.

Frequently asked questions

Is Basic maths easier than Standard?

The syllabus is the same, but the Basic paper has more recall and understanding questions and fewer application and reasoning questions. It is generally the easier paper, but it can limit your Class 11 choices, so check your school's policy before choosing it.

Can I skip a chapter?

It is not a good idea. Every unit carries at least 6 marks, questions from one chapter often use ideas from another, and the MCQs and case studies spread across the whole syllabus. If time is short, cover every chapter to the NCERT level before going deeper into any one.

How many hours a day should a Class 10 student study maths?

There is no single right number. Regularity matters more than hours. For most students, an hour of focused practice on school days, more at weekends, and full timed papers in the final three months is a sound pattern. Adjust it to your own error log: more time where you are losing marks.

Is the second board exam easier?

Nothing announced by CBSE suggests the second exam is easier. It covers the full syllabus. Treat it as a chance to improve, not as an easier route.
