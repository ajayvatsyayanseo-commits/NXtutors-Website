Class 12 chemistry is really three subjects in one paper. Physical chemistry is numerical and formula-driven. Inorganic chemistry is about trends, electronic configurations and reasons. Organic chemistry is about reactions, mechanisms and conversions. Most students are comfortable with one or two of these and quietly avoid the third. That is where marks go missing, because the 2026-27 paper draws on all ten chapters and gives no chapter-wise choice.

This guide is written for the 2026-27 batch, who sit the board exam in 2027. It covers the official chapter-wise weightage, the question paper design, the chapters that are now assessed only formatively, the practical exam, a chapter-by-chapter guide to all ten NCERT chapters with the key reactions and results, a month-by-month plan, a last-30-days checklist, common mistakes, and how to balance the board paper with NEET, JEE and CUET. The syllabus and paper details have been checked against CBSE's 2026-27 curriculum and sample question paper.

CBSE Class 12 Chemistry 2026-27 at a glance

Subject code – Chemistry (043).

Marks – 70 for the theory paper and 30 for the practical exam, 100 in total.

Paper – 3 hours, 33 compulsory questions in five sections (A to E), with internal choice in some questions.

Syllabus – ten chapters from NCERT Chemistry Part I and Part II for Class 12.

No calculators or log tables – the sample paper states that neither is allowed, so numericals are set with manageable numbers.

No change – CBSE's 2026-27 sample paper states there is no change in the question paper design and assessment pattern for this session.

Class 12 has one main board exam; the two-exam system introduced from 2026 applies to Class 10. In 2026, Class 12 practical exams began in January and theory exams began on 17 February. The 2027 date sheet has not yet been published, so take exact dates from cbse.gov.in when they are announced.

What is formative-only or no longer in the Class 12 syllabus

This is the section older guides get most wrong, including an earlier version of this post. The Class 12 chemistry syllabus has changed a great deal in recent years.

Assessed only formatively in 2026-27

The 2026-27 curriculum lists four topics that are "included in the syllabus but will be assessed only formatively to reinforce understanding without adding to summative assessments". CBSE provides the relevant NCERT reading material separately. These will not be tested in the board theory paper:

  • Surface Chemistry: adsorption (physisorption and chemisorption), factors affecting adsorption of gases on solids, the colloidal state, lyophilic and lyophobic colloids, coagulation and types of emulsions.
  • General Principles and Processes of Isolation of Elements: concentration, oxidation, reduction, electrolytic method and refining.
  • Polymers: polymerisation, homopolymers and copolymers with a few examples.
  • Chemistry in Everyday Life: chemicals in medicines (analgesics, tranquilisers, antiseptics, disinfectants, antimicrobials, antifertility drugs, antibiotics, antacids, antihistamines) and chemicals in food (preservatives, artificial sweeteners, antioxidants).

Your school may still teach these and test them in class. Surface chemistry also appears in the practical syllabus (preparing sols, dialysis, emulsions), so do not ignore it for the lab.

Not in the Class 12 syllabus at all

  • The p-Block Elements (Groups 15 to 18) are not part of the current Class 12 syllabus. Older guides that call p-block "high priority" for the board are out of date.
  • The Solid State is not in the current Class 12 syllabus.
  • Environmental Chemistry is not a Class 12 chapter.
  • General organic chemistry (hybridisation, inductive effect, resonance, nomenclature basics) is a Class 11 chapter. You need it as a foundation for every organic chapter, but it is not a separate Class 12 chapter.

Some of these topics remain important for entrance exams. That is covered in the section on NEET, JEE and CUET below.

Sixty per cent of the paper rewards applying and reasoning, not recall. The 2026-27 sample paper shows what this means: a table of boiling points and pKb values for three amines, a log k versus 1/T plot to read the activation energy from, a graph of pKa values of substituted phenols, experimental cell EMF values to compare with the Nernst equation, and a student's Benedict's-test observations to identify a carbohydrate. You need the reactions, but you also need to read data.

How to handle each question type

MCQs – Many test one reaction or one trend. Eliminate options that contradict a known fact; the rest usually becomes clear.

Assertion–reason – Judge each statement separately. In the sample paper, the claim that nitration of aniline gives significant meta product is true, and the reason that –NH₂ is ortho/para directing is also true, but it does not explain the assertion.

2-mark questions – Balanced equations, a short reason, or two one-mark parts. Be precise; one missing reagent can cost half the marks.

3-mark questions – Short numericals (activation energy, colligative properties), name reactions with missing reactants or products, and two-step conversions.

Case-based questions – Read the data or graph carefully; two 1-mark parts and a 2-mark part with choice.

5-mark questions – Often five 1-mark reasoning parts on one chapter (coordination compounds in the sample paper) or a mix of a numerical and reasoning.

Volumetric analysis in the 2026-27 syllabus is the titration of KMnO₄ against a standard solution of oxalic acid or ferrous ammonium sulphate (Mohr's salt), with students preparing the standard solution by weighing it themselves. Salt analysis is the identification of one cation and one anion in a given salt; insoluble salts are excluded. Content-based experiments come from areas such as surface chemistry (sols), chemical kinetics (sodium thiosulphate with HCl), thermochemistry, the Zn/Cu cell, chromatography, preparation of inorganic and organic compounds, functional group tests, and tests for carbohydrates, fats and proteins.

Practise titrations until concordant readings come quickly.

Learn the salt-analysis flow chart and the confirmatory test for each ion.

Know the chemistry behind each functional group test, not only the colour.

Keep the record complete and signed through the year.

Choose a project you actually carry out; the viva will ask about it.

Chapter-by-chapter guide to all ten chapters

For each chapter: what the board tests, the key reactions or results, the questions to expect, and the errors that cost marks.

1. Solutions

What is tested: Types of solutions, concentration terms, solubility and Henry's law, vapour pressure and Raoult's law, ideal and non-ideal solutions, colligative properties with molar mass determination, and abnormal molar masses with the van 't Hoff factor.

Key results: p = K_H x (Henry's law). Relative lowering of vapour pressure equals the mole fraction of solute. ΔT_b = iK_b m, ΔT_f = iK_f m, π = iCRT. Positive deviation (ethanol–water, acetone–CS₂) and negative deviation (acetone–chloroform) with their volume and enthalpy changes. Azeotropes, such as nitric acid with about 68% acid by mass.

Typical questions: Concentration in ppm; molar mass from a colligative property; why a mixture of acetone and CS₂ does not have the expected volume; why an azeotrope cannot be separated by fractional distillation.

Common errors: Using molarity where molality is needed. Forgetting i for electrolytes. Mixing up the signs of ΔV and ΔH for positive and negative deviations. Units of K_b and K_f.

2. Electrochemistry

What is tested: Galvanic cells, electrode potentials, the Nernst equation, conductance and molar conductivity, electrolytic cells and electrolysis, batteries, fuel cells and corrosion.

Key results: E_cell = E°_cell − (0.059/n) log Q at 298 K. ΔG° = −nFE°_cell. Λ_m = κ × 1000/C (with κ in S cm⁻¹ and C in mol L⁻¹). Kohlrausch's law and Λ_m = Λ°_m − A√C for strong electrolytes. Faraday's laws. Reactions in the lead storage battery and the H₂–O₂ fuel cell. Sacrificial protection against corrosion.

Typical questions: Nernst equation calculations; unit conversions for molar conductivity; charge needed to deposit a metal; why magnesium protects iron pipes; electrode reactions during electrolysis; a data-based question comparing measured and calculated EMF.

Common errors: Writing Q upside down in the Nernst equation. Forgetting n. Unit errors between S cm² mol⁻¹ and S m² mol⁻¹. Confusing anode and cathode signs in galvanic and electrolytic cells.

3. Chemical Kinetics

What is tested: Rate of reaction, factors affecting rate, integrated rate equations for zero and first order, half-life, temperature dependence (Arrhenius equation) and collision theory.

Key results: For first order, k = (2.303/t) log([R]₀/[R]) and t½ = 0.693/k. For zero order, t½ = [R]₀/2k. log(k₂/k₁) = (Eₐ/2.303R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). A plot of log k against 1/T has slope −Eₐ/2.303R. Pseudo first-order reactions, such as hydrolysis with water in excess.

Typical questions: Time for 75% completion of a first-order reaction (two half-lives); activation energy from a graph or two temperatures; order versus molecularity; when rate stays constant (zero order).

Common errors: Confusing order with molecularity. Using ln where the formula has log with 2.303. Unit errors in k for different orders. Reading a slope with the wrong sign.

4. The d- and f-Block Elements

What is tested: Position and electronic configuration of d-block elements, general properties of transition elements, important compounds (potassium dichromate and potassium permanganate), lanthanoids, actinoids, and applications.

Key results: Trends in atomic size, ionisation enthalpy, oxidation states, melting points, colour, magnetic moment (μ = √(n(n + 2)) BM), catalytic behaviour, interstitial compounds and alloy formation. Lanthanoid contraction and its consequences. Oxidising reactions of Cr₂O₇²⁻ and MnO₄⁻ in acid medium.

Typical questions: Which ion has the highest magnetic moment; why a metal melts at a lower temperature than its neighbour; balancing permanganate or dichromate redox equations; why transition metals form complexes; comparisons of lanthanoids and actinoids.

Common errors: Writing electronic configurations of ions by removing 3d electrons before 4s. Unbalanced redox equations. Giving a trend without a reason. Forgetting the exceptional configurations of Cr and Cu.

5. Coordination Compounds

What is tested: Werner's theory, key terms (ligand, coordination number, denticity), IUPAC nomenclature, isomerism, bonding (valence bond theory and crystal field theory), bonding in metal carbonyls, and importance and applications.

Key results: Primary and secondary valency. Strong and weak field ligands (the spectrochemical series). Hybridisation and geometry: sp³ tetrahedral, dsp² square planar, d²sp³ and sp³d² octahedral. Crystal field splitting in octahedral and tetrahedral fields, pairing energy and colour. Geometrical and optical isomerism. Synergic bonding in metal carbonyls.

Typical questions: Naming a complex or writing its formula from the name; predicting geometry and magnetic behaviour; which complex is optically active; conductivity of a series of cobalt(III) ammine complexes; why hydrated copper sulphate is blue. The sample paper's Section E long answer is entirely on this chapter.

Common errors: Alphabetical order of ligands ignored in naming. Wrong oxidation state of the metal. Assuming all four-coordinate complexes are tetrahedral. Using VSEPR instead of VBT or CFT for complexes.

6. Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

What is tested: Classification, nomenclature, the nature of the C–X bond, preparation of haloalkanes and haloarenes, physical properties, chemical reactions (nucleophilic substitution, elimination, reactions with metals), and polyhalogen compounds.

Key results: SN1 (carbocation, racemisation, favoured by 3° substrates) versus SN2 (one step, inversion, favoured by 1° substrates). Ambident nucleophiles: KCN gives nitriles and AgCN gives isocyanides. Low reactivity of haloarenes towards nucleophilic substitution, and the activating effect of electron-withdrawing groups at ortho and para positions. Wurtz and Wurtz–Fittig reactions. Saytzeff's rule in elimination.

Typical questions: Predicting mechanism and stereochemistry from optical rotation; which halide hydrolyses more readily (allylic versus secondary); effect of nitro and methoxy groups on chlorobenzene; IUPAC names.

Common errors: Mixing up SN1 and SN2 conditions. Saying haloarenes are more reactive than haloalkanes. Forgetting the ambident behaviour of CN⁻.

7. Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

What is tested: Classification and nomenclature, structures of the functional groups, preparation, properties and reactions of alcohols and phenols, some commercially important alcohols, and ethers.

Key results: Acidity order and the effect of substituents on phenol acidity (electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity; alkyl groups decrease it). Reactions of phenol: Kolbe's reaction, Reimer–Tiemann reaction, bromination and nitration. Dehydration of alcohols. Williamson synthesis and cleavage of ethers by HI. Preparation of alcohols from Grignard reagents and carbonyl compounds.

Typical questions: Choosing the Grignard reagent and carbonyl compound for a given alcohol; acidity comparisons using pKa data (a case study in the sample paper); miscibility of ethers with water; distinguishing tests.

Common errors: Reversing the pKa–acidity relationship (lower pKa means stronger acid). Wrong Grignard combinations. Leaving out reaction conditions such as temperature or catalyst.

8. Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids

What is tested: Nomenclature and structure of the carbonyl and carboxyl groups, methods of preparation, physical properties, chemical reactions and uses. At 8 marks, this is the heaviest organic chapter.

Key reactions: Rosenmund reduction, Stephen reaction, Etard reaction, Gattermann–Koch reaction, nucleophilic addition (HCN, NaHSO₃, alcohols, ammonia derivatives), Clemmensen and Wolff–Kishner reductions, aldol and cross-aldol condensation, Cannizzaro reaction, Tollens' and Fehling's tests, Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky reaction, and decarboxylation. Acidity of carboxylic acids and the effect of substituents.

Typical questions: Identifying name reactions and missing products (aldol, Etard and Wolff–Kishner in the sample paper); why pH around 3.5 is used for adding ammonia derivatives; why dry HCl is used with alcohols; distinguishing tests using NaHCO₃ and 2,4-DNP; orders of reactivity.

Common errors: Aldol attempted on aldehydes without α-hydrogens (they undergo Cannizzaro instead). Missing reagents over the arrow. Confusing Clemmensen (Zn-Hg/HCl) with Wolff–Kishner (NH₂NH₂, KOH, ethylene glycol). Wrong reactivity order of aldehydes and ketones.

9. Amines

What is tested: Structure, classification and nomenclature of amines, preparation, physical properties, chemical reactions, and diazonium salts: preparation, properties, reactions and use in synthesising aromatic compounds.

Key reactions: Gabriel phthalimide synthesis (primary aliphatic amines only), Hoffmann bromamide degradation, carbylamine reaction, Hinsberg's test, acetylation to control the reactivity of aniline, diazotisation, Sandmeyer and Gattermann reactions, replacement by fluorine (Balz–Schiemann), and coupling reactions. Basicity order in gas phase and in water.

Typical questions: Basicity and volatility comparisons from data; two-step conversions (nitrobenzene to fluorobenzene, benzoic acid to benzylamine); why nitration of aniline gives meta product; why acetylation is needed for monosubstitution; which reaction gives a foul-smelling product.

Common errors: Using Gabriel synthesis for aromatic amines. Wrong basicity order of methylamines in water. Forgetting that diazotisation needs 273–278 K. Mixing up Hinsberg results for primary and secondary amines.

10. Biomolecules

What is tested: Carbohydrates, proteins, enzymes, vitamins, nucleic acids and hormones, as listed in the 2026-27 syllabus.

Key results: Classification of carbohydrates; reactions of glucose and the evidence for its cyclic structure; anomers; reducing and non-reducing sugars; sucrose, maltose, lactose, starch, cellulose and glycogen. Amino acids (essential and non-essential, acidic, basic and neutral), peptide bonds, primary to quaternary structure, and denaturation. Vitamins and deficiency diseases. DNA versus RNA and their bases.

Typical questions: Classifying an amino acid from its formula; why milk curdles with lemon juice (denaturation of casein); completing a DNA base sequence; identifying a carbohydrate from test results (a case study in the sample paper).

Common errors: Calling sucrose a reducing sugar. Mixing up fat-soluble and water-soluble vitamins. Pairing adenine with uracil in DNA.

How to study each branch

Physical chemistry: formulas plus units

Keep one formula sheet for Solutions, Electrochemistry and Kinetics with units beside every symbol. Practise numericals without a calculator: the board sets numbers that simplify, so if your arithmetic is getting ugly, check the method. Aim for a few numericals every day rather than a large batch once a week.

Inorganic chemistry: trends with reasons

Inorganic questions ask "why". Learn each trend with its reason: why melting points peak in the middle of the series, why Mn³⁺ is a strong oxidising agent, why lanthanoid contraction makes Zr and Hf so similar. For coordination compounds, practise naming and geometry until they are automatic.

Organic chemistry: reaction maps and conversions

Make one reaction map per chapter, with reagents and conditions written over every arrow. Then practise conversions: two- and three-step routes between functional groups. Learn name reactions with an example each. Mechanisms help you remember, but the board asks mostly for products, reagents, reasons and orders of reactivity.

Step marking and answer presentation

CBSE marks chemistry answers step by step. In numericals, the formula, the substitution and the final answer with units each carry marks. In organic questions, the reagent and conditions are often worth as much as the product. Here is a 2-mark Nernst equation answer set out the way examiners like to see it:

Calculate the EMF of the cell Zn | Zn²⁺ (1.0 M) || Cu²⁺ (0.10 M) | Cu at 298 K, given E°cell = 1.10 V.

Cell reaction: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, so n = 2.

E_cell = E°cell − (0.059/n) log([Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺])

= 1.10 − (0.059/2) log(1.0/0.10) = 1.10 − 0.0295 × 1

E_cell ≈ 1.07 V.

Writing the cell reaction first gives you n and the correct form of Q. That single line prevents the two most common Nernst errors. A lower EMF than E° also makes sense here, because the product ion is more concentrated than the reactant ion.

Presentation rules that protect your marks

Write the formula before substituting, with units for every quantity.

Write reagents above and conditions below every reaction arrow.

Draw structures clearly; a wrong bond position changes the compound.

For "account for" questions, give one clear reason per part, not a paragraph.

Underline the final answer in numericals, with its unit.

Number every answer exactly as in the paper, including sub-parts.

If you attempt both internal choices, cross out the one you do not want marked.

Common mistakes that cost marks (and the fix for each)

Molarity used for molality – Colligative formulas use molality (mol per kg of solvent).

van 't Hoff factor forgotten – Apply i for every electrolyte in colligative problems.

Deviation signs swapped – Positive deviation: ΔV > 0 and ΔH > 0; negative deviation: both negative.

Q inverted in the Nernst equation – Products over reactants, with n from the balanced equation.

Conductivity units wrong – Convert S cm⁻¹ and mol L⁻¹ carefully before using Λ_m = κ × 1000/C.

Order confused with molecularity – Order is experimental; molecularity is for elementary steps only.

log and ln mixed – Use 2.303 log throughout if the formula is written that way.

Ion configurations wrong – Remove 4s electrons before 3d when forming transition metal ions.

Unbalanced redox equations – Balance atoms, then charge, then check.

Trend without reason – Every inorganic "why" needs a reason, not just the order.

Ligands not alphabetical – Name ligands in alphabetical order, ignoring numerical prefixes.

Wrong oxidation state – Work out the metal's oxidation state before naming or predicting magnetism.

All four-coordinate complexes called tetrahedral – Strong field ligands can give square planar geometry.

SN1 and SN2 mixed – Link substrate, solvent and stereochemistry to each mechanism.

KCN and AgCN outcomes swapped – KCN gives nitriles; AgCN gives isocyanides.

pKa read the wrong way – Lower pKa means a stronger acid.

Aldol on aldehydes without α-hydrogen – Those undergo Cannizzaro reaction instead.

Reagents missing over the arrow – Write reagent and conditions for every step.

Gabriel synthesis for aniline – Gabriel gives primary aliphatic amines only.

Diazotisation temperature missing – Write 273–278 K; it is part of the answer.

Sucrose called reducing – Sucrose is non-reducing; maltose and lactose are reducing.

Studying removed chapters – p-block, solid state and surface chemistry are not board theory topics in 2026-27.

Data questions rushed – Read the table or graph twice; the answer is usually in it.

Time lost on long answers – In five 1-mark-part questions, keep each part to two or three lines.

Balancing boards with NEET, JEE and CUET

The Class 12 chemistry syllabus overlaps heavily with NEET, JEE and CUET, but the entrance exams are set by a different body and can cover more.

Board – Rewards balanced equations, conditions, reasons and step-by-step numericals.

NEET – Fast, accurate recall across Class 11 and 12 NCERT, with inorganic and organic facts tested closely.

JEE – Deeper physical chemistry numericals and organic mechanisms, and multi-concept questions.

CUET – MCQs close to NCERT level; board preparation covers most of it.

Syllabus gaps – Topics outside the CBSE Class 12 board syllabus, such as p-block chemistry, may still appear in entrance syllabi. Check the current official syllabus for your exam before dropping any topic.

A practical pattern: learn each chapter to board level from NCERT first, then add entrance-level MCQs in the same week. From December, alternate board papers and entrance practice, because written answers with conditions and reasons are a separate skill. For entrance support, see NEET chemistry tutors.

Frequently asked questions

Is p-block in the CBSE Class 12 syllabus for 2026-27?

No. The 2026-27 Class 12 chemistry syllabus has ten chapters, and p-block elements are not among them. They may still be part of some entrance exam syllabi, so check yours.

Will Polymers and Chemistry in Everyday Life come in the board exam?

No. The 2026-27 curriculum lists Surface Chemistry, Isolation of Elements, Polymers and Chemistry in Everyday Life as assessed only formatively, which means they do not add to the board theory paper. Your school may still teach and test them.

Which branch should I start revising first?

Start with whichever branch is weakest, but do not leave organic chemistry to the end. It carries 33 of 70 marks and needs repeated revision to stick.

Are calculators or log tables allowed?

No. The 2026-27 sample paper states that neither is allowed. Practise numericals by hand so that simplifying logs and powers of ten is routine.

Chemistry rewards organised revision. Keep a formula sheet for physical, a trends sheet for inorganic, and reaction maps for organic; practise papers under time; and read every data question twice. That is how marks are secured.
