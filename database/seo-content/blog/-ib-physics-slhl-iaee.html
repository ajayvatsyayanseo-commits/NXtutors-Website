IB Physics changed a lot with the new guide first assessed in May 2025. The old core-plus-options structure has gone, Paper 3 has gone, and the internal assessment now allows some collaboration. Many revision notes, websites and older textbooks still describe the previous course, so students and parents often plan around a syllabus that no longer exists.

This guide explains the current IB Diploma Physics course as the IB describes it: the five themes and their topics, what is SL and what is HL, the two exam papers and their weightings, the scientific investigation (the IA), the collaborative sciences project and the Extended Essay in physics. It then covers how to choose between SL and HL, common mistakes, using the data booklet, a revision plan and how to pick an IA topic without crossing the academic-honesty line.

The IB Physics course at a glance

The IB recommends 150 teaching hours for an SL subject and 240 hours for HL. In physics, those hours are split between syllabus content and an experimental programme.

The course is built around three concepts that run through every theme: forces, energy and particles. Alongside the content, students must develop skills in the study of physics, grouped as tools (experimental techniques, technology and mathematics) and the inquiry process (exploring and designing, collecting and processing data, concluding and evaluating). The nature of science runs through the whole course as an overarching theme.

The five themes and their topics

The syllabus is grouped into five themes, labelled A to E. Some topics are for all students, some have extra content for HL, and some are HL only.

If you are working from an older book, note what has gone. The previous course had a core, additional HL topics and one option chosen from relativity, engineering physics, imaging and astrophysics. The options have been removed, and some of their content has been folded into the main syllabus at SL or HL. Anything labelled "Option A–D" or "Paper 3" belongs to the old course.

How IB Physics is assessed

All students now sit two external exam papers. Together they make up 80% of the final grade, and the scientific investigation makes up the other 20%. The weightings are the same at SL and HL; HL papers are longer and cover the HL content.

Paper 1 (1A and 1B)

Paper 1A is multiple choice. Paper 1B contains data-based questions, which the IB uses to test skills such as graphing, units and uncertainties. In the IB's specimen papers, Paper 1A is out of 25 marks at SL and 40 at HL, and Paper 1B is out of 20 at both levels. The specimen papers also state that a calculator and a clean copy of the physics data booklet are required. Students who learnt the old course sometimes assume multiple choice is a no-calculator paper; for the new course, check the instructions on the current papers your school gives you.

Paper 2

Paper 2 starts with short-response questions, each focused on a narrow area of the syllabus. It ends with extended-response questions that draw on different areas of the guide and use one of the three concepts (forces, energy, particles) throughout: one such question at SL and two at HL. These are the questions that reward students who can link topics, not just recall them.

The internal assessment: the scientific investigation

The IA is an open-ended investigation in which the student gathers and analyses data to answer a research question they have formulated themselves. It is written up as a report of no more than 3,000 words, is allocated 10 hours in the recommended teaching time, and is worth 20% of the final grade at both SL and HL.

Two changes matter most:

  • Limited collaboration is allowed. Students can work in small groups and share similar methods, as long as the independent or dependent variable differs between students and each student's data is their own. Every student still submits an individual report.
  • Half the marks are for thinking about results. The IB states that 50% of the IA marks are allocated to the conclusion and evaluation. A tidy method with little analysis of what the data means, or of the investigation's weaknesses, will not score well.

Your teacher will share the full marking criteria for the IA; read them before you choose a topic, not after you have collected data.

Picking an IA topic

  • Choose something you can measure well. Strong topics have a clear independent variable you can change over a good range and a dependent variable you can measure with sensible uncertainty, using equipment your school lab actually has or a reliable simulation or database.
  • Link it to physics you can explain. A good IA tests or explores a relationship you can model, such as how damping affects an oscillation or how a resistance changes with temperature. A topic with no clear theory makes the conclusion hard to write.
  • Keep the question narrow. "How does the length of a pendulum affect its period?" is manageable; "What affects pendulums?" is not.
  • Plan for enough data. Aim for enough values of the independent variable, with repeats, to draw a proper graph with uncertainties.
  • Think about the evaluation early. Note the problems and limitations while you work, since evaluation carries so many marks.

The academic-honesty line

The IA must be your own work. A teacher or tutor can explain the physics, suggest reading, ask questions that sharpen your research question, teach you how to propagate uncertainties or draw a best-fit line, and point out what the criteria reward. They should not choose your topic for you, design your method, collect or process your data, or write or rewrite any part of your report. The same applies to AI tools: follow your school's academic integrity policy, including what it says about AI, and cite every source you use. If you share a method with classmates, make sure your variable and your data are genuinely your own.

The collaborative sciences project

The collaborative sciences project is an interdisciplinary project that addresses a real-world problem which can be explored through the different sciences. Students work with those taking other DP science subjects, either in their own school or with other IB World Schools, and the IB recommends 10 hours for it at both SL and HL. It does not appear in the IB's assessment weightings for physics, but it is part of the course, and it builds teamwork and communication skills.

The Extended Essay in physics

The Extended Essay (EE) is a compulsory part of the Diploma core: an independent research essay of up to 4,000 words, written under the guidance of a supervisor. The IB has launched a new EE, first assessed in May 2027, with two pathways: a subject-focused essay (for example, in physics alone) or an interdisciplinary essay that combines two DP subjects.

Under the new model, students have three formal reflection sessions with their supervisor, the last being a short viva voce, and write a 500-word reflective statement. The essay is assessed on framework for the essay (6 marks), knowledge and understanding (6), analysis and line of argument (6), discussion and evaluation (8) and reflection (4). Together with Theory of Knowledge, the EE can earn up to three points towards the diploma.

A physics EE needs a focused research question that can be answered with experimental data, secondary data or simulation within the word limit. It should go beyond an IA: more depth, more reading and a sustained argument. Do not reuse your IA as your EE; check your school's rules on overlap with your supervisor.

SL or HL: how to choose

HL covers 180 hours of content compared with 110 at SL, with HL-only topics such as rigid body mechanics, relativity, thermodynamics, induction and quantum physics, plus extra depth in waves, fields and atomic physics. The papers are longer, and Paper 2 has two extended-response questions rather than one.

  • Take HL if your child enjoys physics, is comfortable with algebra, graphs and trigonometry, and is heading towards engineering, physics or related university courses. Check the entry requirements of the universities and courses they are considering, including whether they ask for HL Physics.
  • SL can be right if physics is a supporting subject, their other HL choices are heavy, or maths is not their strength.
  • Think about maths too. Physics HL leans heavily on mathematical skills. Our guide to IB Maths AA and AI at SL and HL helps with that side of the choice.

Talk to the school's physics teacher before the deadline for changing levels. A realistic look at Grade 10 or IGCSE physics and maths results is usually a good guide.

Common mistakes in IB Physics

  • Revising from the old syllabus. Past papers from before 2025 still help with physics skills, but they include option questions and a different paper structure. Match every resource against the current themes.
  • Ignoring uncertainties. Uncertainties, significant figures and error bars appear in Paper 1B, Paper 2 and the IA. Many students lose marks here.
  • Weak graphs. Missing units, poor scales and unjustified best-fit lines cost marks in data questions.
  • Formula hunting. Picking an equation from the data booklet without understanding when it applies leads to wrong answers that look right.
  • Vague explanations. In "explain" and "outline" questions, examiners want physics reasoning in clear steps, not general statements.
  • Leaving the IA late. Rushed data collection makes a good conclusion and evaluation impossible, and those carry half the IA marks.
  • Treating the extended-response questions as ordinary long answers. They join ideas from different themes, so practise linking topics.

Using the data booklet well

The physics data booklet is required in the exams, so learn to use it long before May.

  • Use it from the first week of the course, not only for revision, so you know where each equation and constant sits.
  • For each equation, know what every symbol means and the units involved. The booklet gives the equation, not the conditions for using it.
  • Know which results are not in the booklet and must be understood or derived, and practise those separately.
  • Always practise past and specimen questions with a clean copy of the booklet, exactly as in the exam.

Frequently asked questions

Is there still a Paper 3 in IB Physics?

No. Since the new course was first assessed in 2025, all students sit two papers: Paper 1 (1A multiple choice and 1B data-based questions) and Paper 2. The option topics that used to be examined in Paper 3 have been removed, with some of their content moved into the main syllabus.

How much is the IB Physics IA worth?

The scientific investigation is worth 20% of the final grade at both SL and HL. It is a written report of up to 3,000 words, and half of its marks are for the conclusion and evaluation.

Can students work together on the IA?

Yes, within limits. Students can collaborate in small groups and share similar methods, but each student's independent or dependent variable must differ, the data must be their own, and each student submits an individual report.

What is the difference between SL and HL Physics?

HL has 180 hours of syllabus content against 110 at SL, including HL-only topics such as rigid body mechanics, relativity, thermodynamics, induction and quantum physics. HL papers are longer, and HL Paper 2 has two extended-response questions rather than one. The weightings of the papers and the IA are the same at both levels.

Can a tutor help with the IA without breaking the rules?

Yes, if they teach rather than do. A tutor can explain the physics and the criteria and teach skills such as uncertainty analysis and graphing. The topic, method, data and writing must be the student's own, in line with the school's academic integrity policy.
