IB Diploma students have to choose between two maths courses, Analysis and Approaches (AA) and Applications and Interpretation (AI), each at Standard Level (SL) or Higher Level (HL). The choice affects university options, how many hours your child spends on maths, and what kind of maths they will be tested on. It is often made quickly at the start of Grade 11, on hearsay about which course is "easier".

This guide explains who each course suits, how SL and HL differ, how the current exams and the internal assessment work (with the numbers checked against the IB's own documents), what is changing in the revised courses first examined in 2029, the topic areas, the mistakes that cost marks most often, a term-by-term revision plan, how to use the graphic display calculator well, and how to approach the exploration honestly.

AA or AI: which course suits which student

Both courses are real, demanding mathematics, and both are offered at SL and HL. The difference is emphasis.

Analysis and Approaches (AA) is built around algebra, functions and calculus, with a strong emphasis on constructing, communicating and justifying mathematical arguments. One of its papers is taken without any calculator. It suits students who enjoy algebraic manipulation and proof, and who are heading towards mathematics, engineering, physics, computer science or economics courses that are mathematically heavy.

Applications and Interpretation (AI) focuses on modelling real situations, statistics and the use of technology. Every paper requires a graphic display calculator, and much of the thinking is about choosing a model and interpreting results in context. It suits students heading towards social sciences, business, psychology, biology, medicine-related or design courses, and students who find algebra-heavy work a barrier but are comfortable with data.

A useful test: give your child a page of algebraic proof and a page of data analysis. The one they would rather spend an evening on is usually a good guide, as long as it also fits their university plans.

SL or HL

The IB recommends 150 teaching hours for an SL subject and 240 for HL. HL is not SL with harder questions; it adds a substantial body of extra content (the IB calls it additional higher level content) and a third exam paper.

In AA HL, the extra content includes complex numbers, proof by induction and by contradiction, vectors, differential equations and Maclaurin series. In AI HL, it includes matrices, vectors, graph theory and differential equations, among other topics. Most IB students take three HL subjects, so choosing maths at HL means it will take a large share of your child's time for two years.

University requirements, in general terms

Universities set their own subject requirements, and they change, so there is no single rule. As a general pattern, mathematically intensive degrees often ask for AA HL, some accept AI HL for certain courses, and many other degrees are satisfied with either course at SL. Before choosing, look up the admissions page for two or three courses your child is seriously considering, in each country they are considering, and check what maths course and level each one asks for. If your child is undecided between a maths-heavy degree and something else, AA keeps more doors open.

How the current courses are assessed

The current courses were first examined in 2021. Students sitting their final exams in 2027 and 2028 follow these courses; check with the school if your child's timeline is different.

Calculators. In AA, Paper 1 is taken with no technology; Paper 2 and Paper 3 require a graphic display calculator (GDC). In AI, a GDC is required on every paper; Paper 1 is short-response questions and Paper 2 extended-response questions. In both courses, SL papers carry 80 marks each and HL Papers 1 and 2 carry 110 each.

Paper format. In AA, Papers 1 and 2 each have a Section A of compulsory short-response questions and a Section B of compulsory extended-response questions. In both courses, HL Paper 3 has two compulsory extended-response problem-solving questions worth 55 marks in total, and usually leads the student through an unfamiliar idea in stages.

The exploration. The internal assessment is an individual written exploration of an area of mathematics, marked out of 20 by the teacher and moderated by the IB. It counts for 20% at both levels. The current guide suggests it should be roughly 12 to 20 pages, double spaced, including diagrams and graphs but excluding the bibliography, and stresses that quality matters more than length. It is assessed against five criteria: presentation, mathematical communication, personal engagement, reflection and use of mathematics. A student who does not submit an exploration does not receive a grade for the course.

Each subject in the Diploma is graded from 1 to 7.

What changes with the revised courses (first assessment 2029)

The IB has published revised subject briefs for both courses with first assessment in 2029, so students starting the Diploma from 2027 will follow the new versions. Both courses stay, both remain at SL and HL, and the overall weightings stay the same: 80% external exams and 20% exploration, with SL taking 3 hours of exams and HL 5 hours.

  • Structure. Each course is organised into five topics. AA: number and algebra, functions, geometry, probability and statistics, and calculus. AI: functions, number and algebra, geometry, probability and statistics, and calculus.
  • Papers. In AA, Paper 1 is still no technology and Papers 2 and 3 require technology. In AI, all papers still require technology. HL Paper 3 remains one hour with two compulsory extended-response questions.
  • The exploration. It is still the mathematical exploration at 20%, but the recommended time rises to 30 hours (the current guide suggests around 10 to 15 hours of teaching time), and the criteria are now the same for SL and HL: problem specification (4 marks), abstraction (6), computation (4) and interpretation (6). These follow the four stages of what the IB calls the mathematical inquiry process.

Full revised guides are available to schools through the IB, so if your child starts the Diploma in 2027 or later, ask the school for the details that apply to them.

Mistakes that cost marks most often

These are patterns worth checking in your child's practice papers.

  • Wrong accuracy. IB papers state that, unless a question says otherwise, numerical answers must be exact or correct to three significant figures. Rounding intermediate values too early is the usual cause of an answer that is "nearly right" and loses the mark.
  • Bare GDC answers. The instructions say that solutions found on a GDC should be supported by suitable working, for example a sketch of the graphs used. A number with no working may earn little, and if it is wrong earns nothing.
  • Ignoring command terms. "Show that" needs every step, because the answer is given. "Hence" means use the previous part. "Write down" means little or no working is needed. Misreading these wastes time or loses method marks.
  • Weak non-calculator algebra in AA. Paper 1 exposes students who rely on the GDC: fraction and surd manipulation, exact trigonometric values, and logarithm laws all need to be fluent by hand.
  • Not interpreting in context. In AI especially, a correct statistic followed by no conclusion, or a conclusion that ignores the context, gives away marks.
  • Units, radians and degrees. A calculator left in the wrong angle mode ruins an entire calculus or trigonometry question.
  • Running out of time in Section B. Students often spend too long on early short questions. Practise with timed papers and move on from a stuck question.

Using the GDC well

For AI, and for AA Papers 2 and 3, the GDC is part of the skill being tested. The IB sets rules on which calculators can be used in exams, so use the model your child's school approves and practise on that exact model from the start of the course.

The functions to master:

  • Graphing, with sensible windows, and finding intersections, zeros, maxima and minima.
  • Solving equations numerically, including ones with no algebraic method.
  • Numerical derivatives at a point and definite integrals.
  • Statistics: summary statistics, regression, and probability distributions (binomial and normal), plus hypothesis tests for AI.
  • Sequences and financial calculations where the course uses them.

Then practise writing down what the calculator did: the equation solved, a sketch of the graph with the point marked, or the distribution and parameters used. That turns a GDC answer into a fully credited answer.

The exploration: practical advice and academic honesty

The exploration is 20% of the grade and entirely within your child's control, which makes it one of the most valuable pieces of work in the course.

  • Choose a focused question, not a broad topic. "Modelling the cooling of tea" is a topic; "Which model best fits the cooling of tea in a paper cup, and how good is Newton's law of cooling here?" is a question with an answer.
  • Use mathematics at the level of the course. The guide says the mathematics should be similar to the syllabus. Going beyond it is not penalised, but copying mathematics your child does not understand shows immediately.
  • Make it personal and genuine. Own data, own decisions and own reflection score better than a textbook topic rewritten.
  • Reflect throughout, not only in the conclusion: why this method, what the limitations are, what would change the result.
  • Keep to school deadlines for the topic, draft and final version.

Where the line is. The IB guide is explicit that the exploration must be the student's own work: the writing and calculations must be their own, sources must be cited, and each student must collect their own data. Teachers point out errors but are not meant to correct them. Authenticity can be checked through the proposal, the draft, the references, the writing style and plagiarism software.

A tutor can teach the mathematics your child needs to understand their chosen area, explain what each criterion rewards, and ask questions that sharpen their thinking. A tutor must not choose the topic, write or rewrite any part, do calculations or analysis, or correct the draft. Any tutor who offers to do more is putting your child's Diploma at risk. Frequently asked questions

Is AI easier than AA?

Not in a simple sense. AI replaces much of the algebra and proof with modelling, statistics and interpretation, which some students find harder. The better question is which course matches your child's strengths and university plans.

Can my child switch from HL to SL, or from AA to AI?

Schools often allow changes early in DP1, subject to timetables and their own policies. Switching later is harder because content and coursework have moved on. Ask the Diploma coordinator early if you are unsure.

How much does the exploration count?

20% of the final grade at both SL and HL, in the current courses and in the revised courses first examined in 2029.

Is there a calculator in every IB maths paper?

Not in AA: Paper 1 is taken without any technology, and Papers 2 and 3 require a GDC. In AI, a GDC is required on every paper.

Is the IB maths syllabus changing?

Yes. The IB has published revised subject briefs for AA and AI with first assessment in 2029. Students examined in 2027 and 2028 follow the current courses. Both courses and both levels continue, and the exploration stays at 20%, with new, common criteria for SL and HL.
